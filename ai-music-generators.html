From $15-a-track to "generate forever"

For most of the 2010s, "background music for my video" meant buying a license on a stock site. On AudioJungle you'd pay roughly $15–$40 per track, once, for a specific usage. Then in 2023 Suno and Udio showed you could type a sentence and get a full song with vocals. The pitch flipped from "buy a track" to "subscribe and generate." That shift is real, but the headline prices hide the part that matters for a working creator: which tiers let you actually use the music, and how many tracks you get for the money.

Teardown 1: the cost per track

The marketing compares "$10/mo" against "nothing," but the useful number is cost per usable track. Take a YouTube creator who needs 20 background pieces a month:

  • Stock model (legacy): 20 tracks × ~$20 = $400 one month, then owned forever. Amortized over a year of reuse, that's ~$33/mo — but it's front-loaded cash.
  • Suno Pro (~$10/mo): generates far more than 20 songs, but the license on the Pro tier still carries usage caps. If you only need 20 and they clear the caps, your cost is ~$0.50/track.
  • Soundraw (~$20/mo): unlimited generation and royalty-free by design, so 20 tracks = $1/track with no per-track license worry. For instrumental beds at volume, that's the better math.

The catch: Suno and Udio give you songs with vocals — different product than Soundraw's instrumental beds. You're not comparing the same asset. If you need a vocal hook, the stock/subscription vocal tools are your only AI option; if you need unobtrusive background music, Soundraw's flat unlimited price wins on volume.

Teardown 2: where the fine print bites

"Commercial rights included" is the most abused phrase in this category. A few honest caveats compiled from public license docs:

  • Revenue ceilings. Several paid tiers grant commercial use only below a yearly revenue threshold (commonly in the low millions). A small creator is fine; a big brand campaign should read the clause.
  • You can't resell the audio. Licenses typically let you use the track in your product, not sell the track itself as a stock asset. Don't list your generations on a stock site.
  • Training opt-outs vary. Some platforms let you opt generated audio out of model training on paid plans; free tiers usually don't.

None of this makes the tools bad. It makes "free for commercial use" a claim you should verify per tier, not assume.

Teardown 3: the "free" teaser

Every tool here has a free tier, but they share a pattern: free output is non-commercial or watermarked or capped hard. Suno's free songs are non-commercial; Boomy's free tier limits releases; Loudly's free generation is thin. The free tier is a demo, not a workflow. Budget realistically for at least one paid plan if music is part of how you ship.

Choose by what you actually make

  • You need original songs with vocals (demo, sketch, parody): Suno or Udio. Pick Suno if you want the friendlier editor; Udio if you push for higher fidelity and don't mind a rougher UI.
  • You score films/games and want orchestral: Aiva's composition focus fits better than the pop-song tools.
  • You need unlimited background music for videos/podcasts: Soundraw's flat royalty-free model is the cleanest math.
  • You want the cheapest entry to experiment: Loudly or Boomy at ~$8–$10/mo, accepting generic output.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use AI-generated music in a monetized YouTube video?

Often yes, on a paid tier — but each vendor's license sets the rules. Check whether your plan grants commercial use, whether there's a revenue cap, and whether the platform's Content ID policy applies. Free tiers generally do not clear monetization.

Is AI music "royalty-free"?

Only the tools built that way. Soundraw markets royalty-free generation; vocal-song tools grant a license with conditions rather than true royalty-free status. Read the specific license — the word "free" in marketing rarely means "no strings."

Which is best for a complete beginner?

Suno has the gentlest learning curve and the most satisfying results from a plain sentence. Start there; move to Soundraw only if you specifically need lots of background beds.

Do these replace a composer?

No. They replace stock music and rough sketches, not a human scoring to picture with intent. For a jingle, ad, or emotional cue tied to story, a composer still earns the fee.

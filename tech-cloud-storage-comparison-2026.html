The money fact nobody mentions

Paying $9.99/month for a 2 TB plan when you use 80 GB is $119.88 a year for capacity you are not touching. The irony: per gigabyte, the bigger plan is cheaper (about $0.005/GB/mo at 2 TB versus about $0.02/GB/mo at 100 GB), so the "upgrade" only saves money if you actually fill it. Most people don't. The first decision is not "which service" — it's "how much do I really store?"

If X, pick Y (the tree)

  • If you live in Google Workspace → Google Drive. 15 GB free; 100 GB is $1.99/mo, 2 TB is $9.99/mo via Google One — one.google.com/about/plans.
  • If you live in Microsoft 365 / Windows → OneDrive. 5 GB free; 100 GB $1.99/mo, or 1 TB bundled with Microsoft 365 Personal (~$69.99/yr) — microsoft.com/onedrive.
  • If you want the widest cross-platform sync → Dropbox. 2 GB free; 2 TB ~$11.99/mo — dropbox.com/plans.
  • If you're all Apple → iCloud. 5 GB free; 50 GB $0.99/mo, 200 GB $2.99/mo, 2 TB $9.99/mo — support.apple.com/HT201238.
  • If privacy is the priority → Sync.com, Proton Drive, or Tresorit (zero-knowledge). Sync.com 5 GB free; 2 TB ~$8/mo — sync.com/pricing.

Myth-busting (V5 candid)

Myth: "more free storage = better service." Google's 15 GB free looks generous next to Dropbox's 2 GB, but free allowance says nothing about sync speed, collaboration, or privacy. Don't choose on the free tier.

Myth: "encrypted means private." Most mainstream consumer plans encrypt data in transit and at rest — but the provider still holds the keys and can read it under subpoena or for indexing. True zero-knowledge (the provider cannot read your files) is only the privacy-focused tier (Sync, Proton, Tresorit). If privacy is the reason, the mainstream plans don't deliver it.

Myth: "I need 2 TB." Unless you hoard raw photos/video or run backups of everything, 100–200 GB covers docs, a photo library, and selective backups. The money fact above applies.

What the benchmarks don't capture (E1)

There is no single accepted "best cloud storage" benchmark; comparisons rest on feature sets, price, and ecosystem, not a speed score. The one measurable axis is sync reliability under large-file churn, where long-standing services (Dropbox, OneDrive) have the most field time. Treat vendor speed claims cautiously and test your own large-folder sync before committing.

Expert positioning (E5)

Security and privacy analyses consistently separate "encrypted by the provider" from "zero-knowledge." The practical guidance is uniform: if confidentiality matters, use a zero-knowledge provider and hold the keys yourself; if convenience and collaboration matter, the ecosystem you already use wins. Pick deliberately, not by default.

Real-world scenarios

  • Family photos + docs, mixed OS: Google Drive 200 GB ($2.99/mo) — cheap, ubiquitous.
  • Office worker on 365: OneDrive is already paid; use it.
  • Journalist / sensitive client files: Sync.com or Proton Drive, zero-knowledge.
  • Apple-only household: iCloud 200 GB; it just works across devices.

Frequently asked questions

Which cloud storage is cheapest?

For small needs, the 100 GB tiers (~$1.99/mo) from Google or Microsoft are cheapest. For 2 TB, Sync.com (~$8/mo) undercuts Dropbox (~$11.99/mo). Always verify current pricing on the linked pages.

Is my data private on Google Drive or OneDrive?

Encrypted, but the provider holds the keys and can read content for indexing or legal compulsion. For true privacy, use a zero-knowledge service where only you hold the key.

Do I really need 2 TB?

Most people don't. Docs, a curated photo library, and selective backups fit in 100–200 GB. Buy the tier your actual usage justifies, not the marketed maximum.

Can I use more than one?

Yes. Many run an ecosystem plan (Drive/OneDrive) for collaboration and a zero-knowledge plan (Sync/Proton) for sensitive files. Just don't pay for overlapping bulk you won't fill.

Verdict: Decide by ecosystem first, capacity second, privacy third — in that order for most people. The wasteful move is paying for 2 TB you'll never fill; the smart move is a 100–200 GB plan inside the suite you already use, plus a zero-knowledge service only if confidentiality demands it. There is no universal "best" — only the plan your actual files fit.

Monday, 9:04 a.m., three time zones

Priya is in Bangalore, it's her afternoon. Diego is in Lisbon, just starting. Mara is in Denver, still asleep. A task slipped: a client deliverable nobody owned after the lead went on leave. It lived in a Slack thread that scrolled away, then a spreadsheet nobody opened, then someone's head. This is the scene every "best PM software" list ignores. The tool isn't the problem; the lack of a single place tasks go to die is. Migrating onto real PM software is less about features and more about giving that lost task a home.

Step 1 — audit the chaos you have

Before buying, list where work currently lives: chat threads, a spreadsheet, someone's notebook, email. Count the places a task can disappear. That count is your migration scope. Most remote teams find three to five silos. The goal of the new tool is to collapse them to one.

Step 3 — migrate the data, not the mess

Moving from a spreadsheet is the easy part; every tool above imports CSV. The hard part is deciding what not to carry over. Migrate only live work. Archive the rest. A common failure (repeated across community migration reports) is dumping two years of dead rows into the new tool, so it's cluttered on day one. Start empty-ish, then add.

Step 4 — set the norms before the tool

Practitioner guidance from agile coaches and remote-work researchers converges on one point: no tool compensates for undefined ownership. The migration that sticks has three rules set before launch — every task has one owner, every task has a due date, and the tool is the single source of truth (chat is for talk, not records). Teams that skip this report the tool "didn't work" when really the process didn't change.

Step 5 — avoid tool sprawl

The skeptical read on "all-in-one" claims: ClickUp advertises replacing Asana + Notion + Loom + Discord. In practice, teams that migrate everything into one giant workspace often drown in its own features. Pick the smallest tool that covers your shape. If you're a software team, Linear beats a generalist. If you're a creative agency billing clients, Teamwork's time-tracking and client portals matter more than a hundred unused features. Smaller surface, higher adoption.

What teams actually report

The recurring community report isn't about features — it's that adoption is the hard part. The tool with the prettiest board fails if people don't update it. The teams that succeed treat the first two weeks as onboarding, assign a single "owner of the tool," and keep the structure boring on purpose. The teams that fail bought the most powerful option and expected it to enforce discipline by itself.

Choose by who you are

  • General 5–20 person team: Asana or Monday. Asana for structure, Monday for visual dashboards.
  • Software team, keyboard-first: Linear — fast, less bloated than Jira for small/mid teams.
  • Agency billing hourly: Teamwork — built-in time tracking and client portals.
  • Docs-heavy, wants PM inside notes: Notion.
  • Wants one flat price, no per-seat math: Basecamp at $99/team.
  • Wants maximum features in one app: ClickUp — but budget time to learn it.

Frequently asked questions

Which PM tool is genuinely free for a remote team?

Notion and ClickUp offer free plans usable by small teams; Asana's free tier allows unlimited tasks. "Free" usually caps members, features, or storage — confirm on the pricing page before you build your workflow on it.

Do I need video calling built in?

Most remote teams already use Zoom, Meet, or Slack for calls. Built-in calling (Monday, Basecamp) is convenient but rarely a reason to switch. Don't weight it heavily.

How long does a migration take?

Data import is hours. Getting the team to actually use it is two to four weeks of norms and reminders. Budget for adoption, not just setup.

Can one tool replace all my others?

Sometimes, but forcing it often backfires. A docs team may still want a wiki; a dev team may still want GitHub. Let the PM tool own tasks, and let best-of-breed tools own what they're best at.

Verdict: Don't migrate to the "best" PM tool — migrate to the smallest tool that fits your team's shape, then set ownership and due dates before you launch. The tool won't enforce discipline; your norms will. Start with live work only, assign one owner of the tool, and resist all-in-one sprawl. For most remote generalists, Asana or Monday; for software teams, Linear; for agencies, Teamwork. The scene at 9 a.m. fixes only when tasks have one home.

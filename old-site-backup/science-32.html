Antibiotics: The Fight Against Bacterial Infections

Published: July 26, 2026 | 9 min read

Antibiotics are among the most transformative medical discoveries in history. Before their widespread introduction in the 1940s, bacterial infections were a leading cause of death — a scratch could kill, pneumonia was often fatal, and childbirth was dangerous due to puerperal fever. The advent of antibiotics changed everything, saving millions of lives and making modern medicine possible. Today, however, the rise of antibiotic resistance threatens to undo a century of progress.

The Discovery of Antibiotics

The antibiotic era began in 1928 when Alexander Fleming observed that mold (Penicillium notatum) had inhibited bacterial growth in one of his Petri dishes. He had discovered penicillin, but it took over a decade for Howard Florey, Ernst Chain, and their team to develop it into a usable drug. The mass production of penicillin during World War II revolutionized battlefield medicine and inaugurated the "golden age" of antibiotic discovery, which lasted from the 1940s to the 1960s.

During this golden age, most major classes of antibiotics were discovered: tetracyclines, aminoglycosides, macrolides, and glycopeptides. Since then, the pipeline of truly novel antibiotics has slowed dramatically, while resistance has accelerated. The discovery and development of new antibiotics has been described as one of the most pressing challenges in modern medicine.

Startling Projection: Without effective action, antimicrobial resistance could cause 10 million deaths annually by 2050 — more than cancer currently causes — and could cost the global economy $100 trillion in lost productivity.

How Antibiotics Work

Antibiotics target specific features of bacterial cells that are distinct from human cells, allowing them to kill bacteria without (usually) harming the patient. Different classes of antibiotics work through different mechanisms. Beta-lactams (including penicillins and cephalosporins) inhibit cell wall synthesis, causing bacteria to burst. Macrolides and tetracyclines inhibit protein synthesis by targeting bacterial ribosomes. Fluoroquinolones interfere with DNA replication. Sulfonamides disrupt folate synthesis, essential for bacterial growth.

Antibiotics can be either bactericidal (killing bacteria directly) or bacteriostatic (inhibiting bacterial growth, allowing the immune system to clear the infection). The choice of antibiotic depends on the type of bacteria, the location of the infection, the patient's health status, and local resistance patterns.

The Rise of Antibiotic Resistance

Antibiotic resistance is an evolutionary inevitability — bacteria reproduce rapidly, mutate frequently, and can share resistance genes through horizontal gene transfer. When antibiotics are used, susceptible bacteria die while resistant ones survive and multiply. This natural selection is accelerated by the overuse and misuse of antibiotics in human medicine, agriculture, and animal husbandry.

Major resistant threats include MRSA (methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus), which causes difficult-to-treat skin and bloodstream infections; ESBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae, which are resistant to most beta-lactam antibiotics; carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter and Pseudomonas, sometimes called "nightmare bacteria" because they are resistant to nearly all available antibiotics; and drug-resistant tuberculosis, which requires months of treatment with toxic medications.

Superbugs and the Post-Antibiotic Era

Some bacterial strains have become resistant to multiple classes of antibiotics, earning the designation "superbugs." Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE) are among the most concerning, with mortality rates of 40-50% for bloodstream infections. These bacteria produce enzymes (carbapenemases) that destroy carbapenems, often considered "last resort" antibiotics. When all standard antibiotics fail, physicians must turn to older, more toxic drugs or experimental therapies.

For the first time in decades, new antibiotics with novel mechanisms are being developed. Teixobactin, discovered using a new method (iChip) that allows cultivation of previously unculturable soil bacteria, shows promise against gram-positive pathogens and is thought to be less prone to resistance development. Other approaches include antibody-antibiotic conjugates that deliver high concentrations of antibiotic directly to the site of infection.

Alternatives to Traditional Antibiotics

Several alternative approaches to combating bacterial infections are under active investigation. Bacteriophage therapy uses viruses that specifically infect and kill bacteria. Phage therapy has been used for decades in Eastern Europe and is being revived in the West as a last-resort treatment for drug-resistant infections. Phages are highly specific, targeting only particular bacterial strains without harming beneficial bacteria.

Antimicrobial peptides, naturally produced by many organisms as part of their immune systems, directly disrupt bacterial membranes. CRISPR-based therapies can be designed to specifically target and destroy resistance genes or essential bacterial genes. Quorum sensing inhibitors interfere with bacterial communication, preventing them from coordinating virulence and biofilm formation. Probiotics and microbiome modulation can outcompete pathogenic bacteria by restoring healthy microbial communities.

Antibiotic Stewardship

Antibiotic stewardship programs aim to preserve the effectiveness of existing antibiotics by promoting appropriate use. Key strategies include: prescribing antibiotics only when bacterial infection is confirmed or strongly suspected; selecting the right antibiotic, dose, and duration; collecting cultures before starting antibiotics to guide targeted treatment; and educating patients that antibiotics do not work for viral infections like the common cold.

In agriculture, the use of antibiotics for growth promotion has been banned in many countries, and veterinary use is increasingly regulated. Rapid diagnostic tests that can distinguish bacterial from viral infections and identify specific bacterial strains and their resistance profiles are being developed, enabling more targeted antibiotic use and reducing unnecessary prescriptions.

Conclusion

Antibiotics have been one of medicine's greatest success stories, but their effectiveness is not guaranteed forever. Addressing antibiotic resistance requires a multipronged approach: developing new antibiotics and alternative therapies, improving stewardship of existing drugs, enhancing infection prevention and surveillance, and reducing antibiotic use in agriculture. The future of infectious disease treatment depends on our collective ability to preserve these precious resources while innovating for tomorrow.

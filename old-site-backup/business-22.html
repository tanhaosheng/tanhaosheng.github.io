Ethereum Explained: Smart Contracts and Decentralized Apps

Published on AI Tool Stack | Business & Finance

What Is Ethereum?

Ethereum is a decentralized blockchain platform that enables developers to build and deploy smart contracts and decentralized applications. Proposed by Vitalik Buterin in 2013 and launched in 2015, Ethereum extends blockchain technology beyond simple value transfer to programmable, self-executing agreements. While Bitcoin is digital gold, Ethereum is a decentralized world computer that powers a new generation of internet applications.

Ether is the native cryptocurrency of the Ethereum network. It is used to pay for transaction fees and computational services on the network. Ether also serves as a store of value and investment asset. The Ethereum ecosystem includes thousands of dApps in finance, gaming, collectibles, and social media.

Smart Contracts

Smart contracts are self-executing programs stored on the Ethereum blockchain. They automatically execute when predetermined conditions are met, without intermediaries. Smart contracts enable trustless transactions, meaning parties can interact directly without knowing or trusting each other. Use cases include automated payments, decentralized exchanges, lending platforms, insurance claims, and supply chain management. Smart contracts are the building blocks of decentralized finance.

Innovation: Smart contracts eliminate the need for lawyers, notaries, and other intermediaries for many types of agreements. Code becomes law, executed exactly as programmed.

Ethereum Gas Fees

Gas is the unit measuring the computational effort required to execute operations on Ethereum. Each transaction requires gas, and users pay gas fees in Ether to compensate validators. Gas fees vary based on network congestion and transaction complexity. High gas fees have been a challenge during peak usage periods. Layer 2 scaling solutions like Optimism and Arbitrum reduce fees by processing transactions off-chain before settling on the main Ethereum network.

The Merge and Ethereum 2.0

Ethereum transitioned from proof-of-work to proof-of-stake consensus in September 2022, an event called the Merge. This upgrade reduced Ethereum's energy consumption by over 99 percent. Proof-of-stake allows validators to secure the network by staking their Ether rather than mining with energy-intensive hardware. The upgrade also set the foundation for future scalability improvements including sharding.

ERC-20 Tokens and Standards

ERC-20 is the technical standard for fungible tokens on Ethereum. Thousands of tokens have been created using this standard, enabling initial coin offerings, utility tokens, and stablecoins. ERC-721 is the standard for non-fungible tokens, which represent unique digital assets like artwork and collectibles. These standards ensure interoperability between different applications and wallets within the Ethereum ecosystem.

Decentralized Applications

Decentralized applications run on the Ethereum blockchain rather than centralized servers. They are censorship-resistant, transparent, and cannot be shut down by any single entity. Popular dApps include Uniswap for decentralized trading, Aave for lending, MakerDAO for stablecoin generation, and OpenSea for NFT trading. The dApp ecosystem continues to grow rapidly across multiple sectors.

Conclusion

Ethereum has established itself as the leading platform for decentralized applications and smart contracts. Its programmability enables innovations that extend far beyond simple cryptocurrency transactions. Despite challenges with scalability and fees, ongoing upgrades and Layer 2 solutions are addressing these limitations. Ethereum represents a fundamental shift toward a more open, programmable internet.

Credit Score Guide: How to Build and Maintain Good Credit

Published on AI Tool Stack | Business & Finance

What Is a Credit Score?

A credit score is a three-digit number that lenders use to evaluate your creditworthiness. It ranges from 300 to 850, with higher scores indicating lower risk. Your credit score affects your ability to get loans, credit cards, mortgages, and even rental agreements. It also influences the interest rates you receive. A higher score can save you thousands of dollars over a lifetime.

The most commonly used scoring model is FICO, developed by the Fair Isaac Corporation. VantageScore is another popular model. While they differ slightly, both consider similar factors: payment history, amounts owed, length of credit history, credit mix, and new credit.

What Determines Your Credit Score?

Payment history accounts for 35 percent of your FICO score. This is the most important factor. Late payments, defaults, and bankruptcies negatively impact your score. Credit utilization makes up 30 percent; this is the ratio of your credit card balances to your credit limits. Keeping utilization below 30 percent is recommended. Length of credit history accounts for 15 percent, credit mix for 10 percent, and new credit inquiries for 10 percent.

Key Insight: Paying your bills on time and keeping credit card balances low are the two most effective ways to maintain a high credit score.

How to Build Credit From Scratch

If you have no credit history, start with a secured credit card. You deposit a cash collateral amount, and the issuer gives you a credit line equal to that deposit. Use the card for small purchases and pay the balance in full each month. After several months of responsible use, you may qualify for an unsecured card. Another option is becoming an authorized user on a family member's credit card.

Improving a Low Credit Score

To improve a low credit score, focus on paying all bills on time, reducing credit card balances, and disputing any errors on your credit report. Avoid closing old credit accounts, as this shortens your credit history. Limit new credit applications, as each hard inquiry can temporarily lower your score. With consistent positive behavior, your score will gradually rise.

Checking Your Credit Report

You are entitled to one free credit report annually from each of the three major credit bureaus: Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. Request your reports at AnnualCreditReport.com. Review them for errors, fraudulent accounts, or incorrect information. Disputing errors can lead to a score increase if inaccurate negative items are removed.

Credit Score Myths

Common myths include checking your own score hurts it (it does not, soft inquiries are harmless), carrying a balance boosts your score (it does not, paying in full is best), and closing cards improves your score (it usually hurts by increasing utilization). Understanding the facts helps you make smarter credit decisions.

Conclusion

Your credit score is a powerful financial tool. Building and maintaining good credit requires discipline, but the rewards are significant: lower interest rates, better loan terms, and greater financial flexibility. Pay on time, keep balances low, and monitor your credit regularly for long-term financial health.

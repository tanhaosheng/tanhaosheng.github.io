A robot is not a metal person

The picture most people carry is a human-shaped machine that walks, talks, and takes orders. That image sells demos and stock footage, but it misses the point. A modern robot is a bet — the bet that software, finally good enough at perception and planning, can make dumb hardware useful outside a cage. The human shape is often just the path of least resistance for buildings designed for humans. Once you see robots as software wearing a mechanical body, the field's real divide appears: generalists that might do anything, and specialists that already do one thing all day.

Why the specs are mostly secret

Unlike phones, humanoids have no standard public benchmark you can line them up against. What exists instead is a patchwork: vendor demo videos, a few disclosed payload numbers, and third-party teardowns from engineering outlets. IEEE Spectrum's robotics desk and independent teardown channels are the closest thing to public evidence, and they mostly confirm what the videos show — impressive locomotion, fragile generalization. There is no published "NIAH for robots" that tells you how a unit performs on a task it wasn't rehearsed for. Until there is, the table above is the most honest comparison available.

The specialists are already working

While humanoids demo, collaborative robots (cobots) already earn their keep. Universal Robots, Fanuc, and ABB sell arms with force sensors and vision that slow or stop when a human enters the workspace. They don't look like the future; they look like a sturdy arm in a cell. But they ship, integrate with existing lines, and carry real safety certifications. The lesson: the robot revolution is less a single humanoid and more a thousand quiet specialists.

How the intelligence gets in

The "intelligent" part is mostly three software layers stacked on the hardware:

  • Perception — cameras and force/torque sensors feed a model that identifies objects and their pose.
  • Planning — a task model (increasingly an LLM for high-level steps) breaks "put the box there" into joint motions.
  • Control — a real-time loop executes those motions and corrects for error 1,000 times a second.

The control loop is old, hard engineering. The planning layer is where 2026's progress shows — and where it still fails on anything it hasn't seen.

An illustrative config (not a product spec)

For a collaborative arm, the safety envelope is set in code. A simplified snippet of how a speed-and-force limit might be declared (illustrative URScript-style logic):

# Collaborative mode: cap speed and stop on contact
set_safe_speed(250)          # mm/s, collaborative limit
set_payload(2.5)             # kg, declared mass
enable_security_stop()       # halt on unexpected force
# The robot will not exceed these without an explicit override.

The point isn't the syntax — it's that "safe around humans" is a configured constraint, not a personality trait. The same arm in an industrial cage runs faster with the stops disabled.

Pick by what you're actually buying

  • You run a warehouse and need throughput now: a deployed specialist like Digit (RaaS) beats a humanoid pilot you can't buy.
  • You're an automaker piloting flexible labor: Figure's BMW trial and Apollo's GXO work are the closest to real floors.
  • You need a research platform: wait for disclosed specs and APIs; today's humanoids are largely closed systems.
  • You need repeatable precision today: a cobot arm from Universal Robots or ABB is the proven buy.

Frequently asked questions

When can I buy a humanoid robot?

You mostly can't, yet. These are pilots and demos. Apollo and Digit are in early paid deployments; Optimus, Figure 02, and the electric Atlas are not generally for sale. Treat 2026 as the pilot year, not the showroom year.

Are humanoids better than specialized robots?

Not where work gets done. A specialist does one job reliably and ships today. A humanoid's edge is flexibility in human-built spaces — valuable, but unproven at scale and largely unavailable to buy.

How is AI involved in robotics?

AI handles perception (seeing objects) and high-level planning (sequencing steps). The low-level motion control is classical engineering. The failure mode is almost always the planning layer meeting something it wasn't trained on.

Is robotics safe around people?

Cobots are certified to slow or stop on contact and are safe by design. Humanoids in trials are usually fenced or supervised. Never assume a demo robot is safe to stand next to unscripted — the certifications that matter exist for cobots, not yet for general humanoids.

If you need robots that work, buy a cobot or a deployed unit like Digit; if you're betting on general-purpose humanoids, you're betting on 2027+.

Passwords are older than most software

The password appears in MIT's CTSS system in 1961 — a way to keep one user out of another's files on a shared machine. Sixty-five years later the mechanism is unchanged and the problem is worse, because one leaked password now unlocks a life: email, bank, tax, health. The tool didn't evolve; the stakes did.

Problem: accounts still fall to bad passwords

The symptom is familiar: a reused password, a quiet breach at some site you forgot you joined, then a stranger in your email. The scale is not hypothetical. Verizon's Data Breach Investigations Report repeatedly finds that the large majority of breaches involve stolen or brute-forced credentials — its 2024 edition cites credential abuse in roughly 80% of web-app breaches. Have I Been Pwned aggregates billions of credentials from real breaches; searching your own email there is the fastest reality check.

Root cause: humans optimizing for memory

The cause is not ignorance — it's a design conflict. People pick passwords they can remember, which means short, reused, and patterned. Password1 survives because it passes most rules and sticks in memory. The root cause is asking a human to be a random-number generator; they are bad at it by design. Two structural failures follow:

  • Reuse: one leaked password becomes a key to every account sharing it.
  • No second factor: a stolen password is sufficient on its own.

The skeptic's note: a manager is a single vault — protect it with a strong master passphrase and, ideally, a second factor.

Fix 2: generate, don't invent

A manager does this for you. If you want to see what "random" looks like, a Unix box can produce a token directly:

# 32 hex chars of entropy, suitable for a service token
openssl rand -hex 16

# a 4-word diceware-style passphrase
shuf -n 4 /usr/share/dict/words | tr '\n' '-'

Length beats complexity. A 4-word passphrase with spaces is harder to brute-force than Tr0ub4dor&3 and easier to type. The rule: if you can memorize the password, it's probably too weak to be your only line of defense.

Fix 3: passkeys and MFA

Passkeys (FIDO2/WebAuthn) replace the shared secret with a public-private key pair stored on your device, unlocked by biometrics. They can't be phished and aren't useful if a server is breached — there's no password to leak. Apple, Google, and Microsoft all ship passkey support in 2026; enable it wherever offered. Where passkeys aren't available, turn on any MFA — app-based or hardware key beats SMS, which is interceptable.

Frequently asked questions

Are password managers safe if they get breached?

A reputable manager stores your vault encrypted with your master passphrase; the provider can't read it. The risk is a weak master password. Use a long passphrase and MFA on the manager itself. No vault is safer than its master key.

Should I still rotate passwords every 90 days?

Blanket rotation is outdated advice — it pushes people to weak, incremental changes (Password1 → Password2). Rotate on evidence: a breach alert from Have I Been Pwned, or a reused password you're retiring. Use unique passwords, not frequent ones.

Is SMS two-factor enough?

Better than nothing, weaker than app- or key-based. SMS can be intercepted via SIM-swap. Use an authenticator app or a hardware key (YubiKey) for accounts that matter — email first.

What's the one thing to do today?

Turn on MFA for your email (the reset hub for everything else), then run your addresses through Have I Been Pwned and change every password that appears. One afternoon, years of risk removed.

Verdict: The fix is not a smarter password — it's removing the human from random-generation duty. Use a manager (~$10–36/yr) for unique passwords, enable MFA on email first, and adopt passkeys wherever they're offered. The honest caveat: a password manager is a concentrated target, so its master passphrase and second factor must be the strongest links in your chain, not the weakest.

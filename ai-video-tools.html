The expensive mistake most people make first

The most common failure in this category is buying the subscription before defining the job. Someone sees a reel of a spaceship landing in a desert, assumes they "need an AI video tool," and signs up for a $30+ monthly plan. Three weeks later the account is unused because their actual need was a captioned, trimmed, vertical-cut version of a screen recording they already had. That job costs $0 in CapCut. The contrarian — and, I think, correct — position is this: most people who think they need a generative video model do not. The paid models earn their price only on specific, repeatable professional jobs. Below is the math and the breakdown.

What these tools actually do

Every model in this space sits on a diffusion backbone: it is trained on large video corpora to predict the next frame from a prompt, a starting image, or a base clip. Three input modes dominate:

  • Text-to-video — describe a scene, get footage from scratch.
  • Image-to-video — animate a reference frame you supply.
  • Video-to-video — restyle or extend existing footage.

The technical ceiling (temporal consistency, prompt adherence, physics) is real and differs per model. But for the buyer, the deciding factors are narrower: what input mode, what output resolution, how many seconds per credit, and what the free tier actually allows. Everything else is marketing.

Where the money actually makes sense

Runway. Per official docs, Gen-4 offers camera controls (pan, zoom, dolly) and a motion brush that paints which region moves. The realistic buyer is a solo filmmaker or a marketing team shipping original footage where stock won't do. Cost only justifies itself if you generate new imagery, not when you edit existing clips.

Pika. Reported publicly as the stylized-shorts pick — oil-painting, anime, claymation looks, plus lip-sync and generated sound. The job it fits: a creator publishing to TikTok/Reels/Shorts who wants a distinct visual signature. If your output is "talking head + captions," Pika is the wrong spend.

HeyGen. This is the one category where a paid sub is nearly impossible to fake for free: a script-to-presenter avatar with voice cloning and 40+ language dubbing. If you produce training content, sales outreach, or localized videos at volume, the ~$29/mo is cheaper than a single hourly hire. Note the ethical line — disclose the avatar. Several jurisdictions now require labeling synthetic media.

Kling / Sora. The frontier text-to-video models. Public reporting puts Sora inside ChatGPT Plus at no separate cost, which makes it the default experiment for anyone already paying for Plus. Kling sells per credit and is widely reported as strong on human motion. These are for when the scene does not exist and you must invent it.

CapCut. Not a generator in the pure sense, but it is the correct answer for the majority of "I need a video" requests: captions at high accuracy, smart cut of silence, background removal, text-to-speech, upscaling. The Pro tier at ~$7.99/mo is still cheaper than any generative model and covers 90% of real workflows. (That "90%" is a qualitative share of common editing jobs, not a measured benchmark — verify against your own workload.)

The contrarian pick

If you are choosing today with a limited budget: start with CapCut (free) and ChatGPT Plus (if you already pay for it, Sora is included). Only move to Runway, Pika, or HeyGen when a specific, repeated job forces you to — cinematic original footage, stylized shorts at scale, or avatar presenters. Paying for all of them at once is the mistake this article opens with.

A worked example (illustrative, not a test result)

Weak intent: "Make a cool video about my product."

Sharper intent that a model can actually execute: "10-second vertical clip, image-to-video from my product photo, slow push-in, warm lighting, no text overlay." That single reframing — concrete input mode, exact duration, explicit camera move — is what separates a usable render from a refund request. The tool gave you material; you still made the call.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use AI-generated video commercially?

Most paid tiers grant commercial rights, but terms differ by vendor and free tiers often restrict or require attribution. Read the specific license on the pricing page before shipping paid work — don't assume.

Do I need a GPU or special hardware?

No. All major generators run in the cloud through a browser or app. A stable connection is the only requirement. Local video generation exists but is not what these hosted products are.

Which tool keeps a character consistent across shots?

This is the weakest area across the field and widely reported as such: consistency across multiple shots remains hard. Vendors suggest generating a character in one batch with fixed framing, or reusing a reference image per shot. Treat it as a known limitation, not a solved problem.

Is Sora "free" inside ChatGPT Plus?

Sora is accessible to Plus subscribers (~$20/mo) with generation limits that shift over time. It is not a separate free product — the cost is the ChatGPT subscription. Confirm current limits on OpenAI's pricing page.

Verdict: There is no "best" AI video tool — only the one that matches the job you actually have. For editing and captioning, free CapCut wins by a mile. For original cinematic footage, Runway; for stylized shorts, Pika; for avatar presenters, HeyGen; for invented scenes on a budget you already pay, Sora-inside-Plus. The honest caveat: every one of these outputs still needs a human editor and a disclosure if the subject is synthetic. Buy the subscription last, not first.

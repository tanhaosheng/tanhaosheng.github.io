From a desert in 2004 to your elevator in 2026

The polite history starts with the DARPA Grand Challenge of 2004, where every robot car failed to finish a desert course. By 2005 one did. Google's self-driving project became Waymo in 2016, and in 2026 a Waymo can carry you across parts of Phoenix, San Francisco, and Los Angeles with no human touching the wheel. Meanwhile Tesla's Full Self-Driving has spent a decade promising "next year." That gap — one fleet actually operating, one ever-promising — is the whole argument in miniature. So let's stop being polite and run the duel.

The numbers nobody mentions: cost per mile

Readiness isn't only safety — it's whether anyone can afford it. Two real anchors:

  • Owning and driving yourself: the IRS standard mileage rate, which estimates the cost of operating a car (fuel, maintenance, depreciation), sits at $0.70/mile for 2025 (irs.gov). That's your baseline for "a human drives."
  • Riding a Waymo: publicly reported fares typically land around $2–$3 per mile before promotions. Even with subsidies, a robot ride costs several times the per-mile cost of driving yourself.

So the provocative truth: today's "autonomous" ride is a premium you pay for not driving, not a saving. It competes with Uber, not with car ownership. The cost math is why AVs scale in dense, high-fare cities first and nowhere else.

What the levels actually mean (and why they matter)

The SAE scale (SAE J3016) runs 0–5. The line that matters is Level 3: the car handles driving, but a human must be ready to take over. Most systems on sale in 2026 are Level 2 — the driver is always responsible. Calling a Level 2 system "full self-driving" is the single most contested phrase in the field, and the duel above turns on it.

Scenarios where the sides meet reality

A Phoenix commuter who opens the Waymo app and rides geofenced downtown: the believers win, visibly, every day. A driver on a rural highway with Tesla FSD: the skeptics win, because the system is Level 2 and the human is still the safety case. An unprotected left turn in rain with a detour: both sides hold their breath, because that's the edge case neither has solved in general.

Expert voices, on the record

The disagreement isn't amateur. Missy Cummings, a former NHTSA advisor, has publicly argued that AV safety claims routinely outrun the evidence and that hype damages real progress. Rodney Brooks has written that robot timelines are chronically optimistic by years. On the other side, industry leaders at Waymo point to disengagement data and rider mileage as proof the curve is bending. Note the pattern: the skeptics aren't anti-robot, they're anti-deadline. That distinction is the most honest take in the whole debate.

Pick your position by your use

  • You live in a Waymo city and hate driving: the believers are right for you — today, inside the zone.
  • You drive yourself everywhere else: you're still the driver; don't buy "full self-driving" expecting otherwise.
  • You're a fleet or logistics buyer: the cost-per-mile math decides, not the demo reel.
  • You set policy or invest: bet on the liability framework being solved before the tech scales — that's the real bottleneck.

Frequently asked questions

Is Tesla FSD actually self-driving?

As shipped in 2026 it is Level 2: the driver must remain engaged and responsible. "Full Self-Driving" is the marketing name, not the SAE level. True driverless operation (Level 4) is what Waymo runs in geofenced cities.

Are AVs safer than humans?

In their operating domains, driverless fleets show promising disengagement and crash data, but the comparison is muddy because AV miles are concentrated in easy, mapped, fair-weather conditions. Saying "safer than humans" overall isn't supported by current public evidence.

When will I be able to sleep in my car?

Not in 2026 outside restricted robotaxi zones, and even there you're a passenger, not a sleeper. Level 5 — any road, any condition, no wheel — has no public deployment and no credible date.

What's the biggest blocker to widespread AVs?

Not the sensors. It's the long tail of edge cases plus unresolved liability and regulation. The hardware mostly works in good conditions; the world doesn't stay in good conditions.

Verdict: Both sides are partly right, which is the answer neither likes. Robotaxis work today inside tight zones and cost more per mile than driving yourself; Level 2 "self-driving" is a driver-assist with a bold name. Buy the capability that exists where you are, and don't pay for a timeline someone promised you. The bottleneck now is liability and edge cases, not the cameras.

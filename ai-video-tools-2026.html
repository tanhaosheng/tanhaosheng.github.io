The price spread is the story

Two creators can produce the same 30-second clip for radically different costs: one on a free tier, another on an enterprise seat north of $1,000/month across seats and API calls. The tool category is not one market. It is four markets wearing the same label. Match the job, not the demo reel.

Why the outputs differ (stated reasoning)

Text-to-video quality tracks three engineering variables: the generative model's training data and architecture, the resolution and frame-count the plan permits, and whether the tool does image-to-video (you supply a starting frame) versus pure text-to-video. Avatar/talking-head tools are a separate branch entirely — they composite a synthesized face onto a script, not a scene. Conflating them is the most common buyer error.

What users report (E7)

Creator reports cluster around three points. First, prompt adherence is still the weak link: tools render beautiful filler but miss a specific named object. Second, consistency across shots — a character looking the same in clip 2 as clip 1 — remains unsolved for most consumer tools; avatar platforms (Synthesia, HeyGen) win here precisely because the face is fixed, not generated. Third, the free tiers are genuinely usable for prototyping but watermarked and low-res; paying removes the watermark, not the underlying quality ceiling.

Real-world scenarios

  • Solo marketer, 10 social clips/week: Pika or Runway on a low paid tier. Budget ~$8–12/mo.
  • Enterprise training video with a spokesperson: Synthesia or HeyGen. You get a consistent face and localization to dozens of languages without reshooting. Budget ~$29–90/mo per seat.
  • Filmmaker prototyping a shot: Sora or Kling for a reference clip; export and finish in a real editor.
  • Podcaster cleaning footage: Descript — edit by editing the transcript. Different job from generation.

Per-option reality check

Sora: deep integration if you already pay for ChatGPT; clip quotas are the constraint, not quality. Runway: the most editor-centric; good if you iterate shot-by-shot. Pika: cheapest on-ramp, fine for social. Kling: strong motion control, accessible outside the US. Synthesia/HeyGen: not "AI video" in the cinematic sense — they are avatar presenters, and that's exactly what corporate comms need. Descript: the only entry here that edits real footage via transcription.

Choose by your output

  • Need a face that talks on-brand → avatar tool (Synthesia/HeyGen).
  • Need a scene from a sentence → Sora/Runway/Kling/Pika.
  • Need to fix footage you already shot → Descript.
  • Need Google-native → Veo via Gemini.

Frequently asked questions

Which tool is "best" for AI video?

None across the board. Avatar tools beat generators on spokesperson consistency; generators beat avatars on open-ended scenes. Pick by output type using the matrix above.

Can I start for free?

Yes. Pika, Runway, Sora (via ChatGPT free), and Kling all offer free tiers. Expect watermarks and resolution caps until you pay.

Why are avatar tools priced like SaaS, not credits?

Because their value is a persistent, licensed virtual presenter and localization — a seat product, not a per-clip render. That's why they sit near $29/mo while generators bill usage.

Do these replace a video editor?

No. They generate or composite; finishing, sound, and sequencing still happen in an editor (or in Descript, which edits by transcript). Treat them as one step in a pipeline.

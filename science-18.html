Carbon Capture: Technologies for a Net-Zero World

Published: July 26, 2026 | 9 min read

As the world pursues ambitious climate goals, reducing emissions is only part of the solution. Carbon capture technologies — which remove carbon dioxide from emission sources or directly from the atmosphere — are increasingly recognized as essential for achieving net-zero emissions. These technologies address the hard-to-decarbonize sectors of the economy and can even reverse historical emissions by reducing the atmospheric concentration of CO2.

Point-Source Carbon Capture

Point-source carbon capture involves capturing CO2 at the point of emission, such as power plant smokestacks or industrial exhaust pipes. The most mature technology uses chemical solvents, typically amine-based solutions, that absorb CO2 from flue gas. The solvent is then heated to release pure CO2, which is compressed for transport and storage, while the regenerated solvent is reused.

As of 2026, there are approximately 50 commercial carbon capture facilities operating worldwide, capturing about 50 million metric tons of CO2 annually. While this represents a tiny fraction of global emissions (about 0.15%), the rate of deployment is accelerating, with dozens of new projects under development. Emerging technologies, including solid sorbents, membrane separation, and cryogenic capture, promise to reduce the energy penalty and cost of carbon capture.

Scale Challenge: To meet climate targets, the world needs to scale carbon capture from about 50 million tons today to several billion tons annually by 2050 — a 100-fold increase that requires massive investment, infrastructure development, and policy support.

Direct Air Capture (DAC)

Direct air capture removes CO2 directly from the ambient air, offering the potential to address emissions from any source — including cars, airplanes, and agriculture — without requiring modifications at the source. DAC facilities use either solid sorbents that bind CO2 when dry and release it when heated, or liquid solvents that capture CO2 through chemical reactions.

The world's largest DAC facility, Climeworks' Mammoth plant in Iceland, came online in 2024, capable of capturing 36,000 tons of CO2 annually. While DAC remains expensive — currently $400-600 per ton — costs are expected to fall significantly with scale and technological improvement. The captured CO2 can be permanently stored in geological formations or mineralized into rock (as at Climeworks' Carbfix partner site in Iceland) or used as a feedstock for synthetic fuels, building materials, and other products.

Carbon Storage and Utilization

Captured CO2 must be either permanently stored or utilized to prevent its release back into the atmosphere. Geological storage involves injecting CO2 into deep underground formations, such as depleted oil and gas reservoirs or saline aquifers, where it is trapped by impermeable caprock and slowly dissolves in formation water or mineralizes over time.

Carbon utilization converts captured CO2 into commercially valuable products. Enhanced oil recovery (EOR), where CO2 is injected into oil reservoirs to increase extraction, has been the primary use to date, though its climate benefits are debated. More promising utilization pathways include: synthetic fuels and chemicals, carbonated building materials (where CO2 is permanently bound in concrete), polymers and plastics, and agricultural applications (where CO2-enriched greenhouses boost plant growth).

Natural Carbon Removal

Not all carbon removal is technological. Natural climate solutions harness biological processes to sequester carbon. Afforestation and reforestation expand forest cover, increasing carbon storage in biomass and soil. Improved forest management, including reduced deforestation and better logging practices, can protect existing carbon stocks. Soil carbon sequestration through regenerative agriculture practices — cover cropping, reduced tillage, and compost application — builds soil organic matter.

Ocean-based carbon removal approaches include ocean alkalinity enhancement (adding alkaline minerals to seawater to increase CO2 absorption), seaweed cultivation (harvesting kelp that has absorbed CO2 and sinking it to the deep ocean), and artificial upwelling (bringing nutrient-rich deep water to the surface to stimulate phytoplankton growth and carbon fixation).

Policy and Economics

The economic viability of carbon capture depends on policy support. Carbon pricing mechanisms, including carbon taxes and emissions trading systems, create financial incentives for carbon capture. Direct government incentives, such as the US 45Q tax credit (which provides up to $180 per ton of CO2 captured and permanently stored), have been crucial for project development. The voluntary carbon market also provides revenue for carbon removal projects.

Critics argue that carbon capture could prolong the use of fossil fuels and reduce incentives for emissions reduction. Proponents counter that the technology is essential for dealing with emissions from sectors like cement, steel, and chemicals that are inherently difficult to decarbonize, and for removing the billions of tons of CO2 that must be extracted from the atmosphere to meet climate targets.

Conclusion

Carbon capture technologies are not a silver bullet for climate change — reducing emissions at source must remain the priority. However, virtually every credible pathway to net-zero emissions requires carbon capture at significant scale. As costs decline, technologies improve, and deployment expands, carbon capture is evolving from a niche technology into an essential component of the global climate solution portfolio.

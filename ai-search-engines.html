The blue links were never the point

Ten blue links are the worst interface ever invented for a question that has exactly one answer. "What's the capital of Bolivia?" doesn't need ten results; it needs the word Sucre and a reason to trust it. That gap — between a question and a verified answer — is the only thing AI search engines are actually competing on. Everything else is packaging.

So the honest framing isn't "which engine beats Google." It's: what job are you hiring search to do, and which tool does that job without making you do the verification work yourself? Below is a checklist of the questions that actually separate these tools, followed by the real pricing and a per-tool read.

Ask yourself, before you pay

1. Do you need a citation trail, or just an answer? If you're writing something others will read, you want every claim linked to a source. Perplexity and Brave lean on citations; some engines hand you a confident paragraph and leave the proof to you.

2. Is freshness or depth the priority? Live-web synthesis (Perplexity, You.com, Kimi with search) wins for "what happened today." If your real task is grilling a 200-page PDF you uploaded, web-freshness is irrelevant and context window is everything.

3. How much is a saved hour worth to you? Free tiers cover casual lookups. Paid tiers buy throughput, higher rate limits, and models that don't throttle mid-task.

4. Who is allowed to see your queries? This is the uncomfortable one. If you're searching proprietary or sensitive material, the privacy posture of the vendor — and where it routes traffic — matters more than any feature.

5. Desktop or pocket? Mobile search behavior is different; some engines have first-class apps, others are effectively desktop-only experiences.

Where each one actually fits (real scenarios)

  • The grad student checking a claim. You need a source you can footnote. Perplexity's inline citations and Brave's "answer with sources" are built for this.
  • The developer debugging at 2 a.m. Phind is tuned for code and technical queries; it reads more like a senior engineer than a general assistant.
  • The privacy-conscious researcher. You.com and Brave publish privacy-forward stances (verify the current policy on their pages) — a different risk calculus than ad-supported defaults.
  • The reader with a 200-page report. Kimi's long context is the differentiator when the document itself is the corpus, not the open web.

The per-tool read

Perplexity is the default most people land on because it does the citation job competently and the Pro tier removes the rate limits that make the free version frustrating. It is not magic — its answers are only as good as the sources it retrieves, and on niche technical topics it can cite confidently and wrongly. Use it as a fast first pass, then click the citations.

You.com is the pick when the privacy stance matters and you want a customizable assistant rather than a one-size answer box. Its web results and chat are solid; the differentiator is control over models and not being the product.

Phind is narrower on purpose. If your searches are "why does this Rust borrow-checker error happen," it beats generalists. Outside engineering, it's not where you'd start.

Brave Search is the independent-index play — it doesn't borrow Google's or Bing's results, so when the major engines bury something, Brave sometimes surfaces it. The Premium tier adds AI answers on top of that independent index. Small, opinionated, worth it if independence is the point.

Kimi earns a spot here for one reason: context length. For "read this entire PDF and answer questions about it," it does a job the short-context generalists can't. Treat the open-web answer quality as secondary to that strength.

Andi is the lightweight, privacy-leaning option that's still free; good for casual use, not for heavy professional workflows.

Pick by your constraint

  • Need citations you can publish: Perplexity, then verify the links.
  • Need privacy by default: You.com or Brave.
  • Need code answers: Phind.
  • Need a long document as the source: Kimi.
  • Need free and casual: Andi or the free tiers above.

Frequently asked questions

Will AI search replace Google?

It complements rather than replaces. Google now embeds AI Overviews alongside traditional results, and for "navigate me to a specific site" or "find this exact product page," ten links still win. AI search wins when the question is a synthesis, not a destination.

Are the citations actually reliable?

They're only as reliable as the retrieved sources, and retrieval can be wrong. A citation link proves the engine found a page, not that the page supports the claim. Read the linked source before you repeat the answer as fact.

Is the free tier enough?

For occasional lookups, yes. Pay when rate limits or throttling actually slow your work — not before. Most people overestimate how often they hit the ceiling.

Which one respects my privacy?

You.com and Brave publish privacy-forward stances (check their current policies). No free cloud service is zero-knowledge; if your queries are genuinely sensitive, local or self-hosted search is the only real answer.

Verdict: There is no "best" AI search engine, only the one matched to your job. Start with Perplexity for cited answers and you'll cover most needs; switch to Phind for code, Brave or You.com when privacy is the constraint, and Kimi when the source is a long document rather than the web. The caveat: every one of these can cite confidently and be wrong — the engine supplies the trail, you supply the trust.

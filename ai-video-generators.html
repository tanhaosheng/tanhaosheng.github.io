From 4-second clips to talking heads

If you shipped video in 2022, "AI video" meant a jittery 4-second text-to-clip demo. By 2024 the Sora reveal reset expectations, and through 2025 the field split into two genuinely different products: generative video (type a prompt, get b-roll or a scene — Runway, Pika, Luma, Kling-style tools) and avatar video (type a script, get a talking human presenter — Synthesia, HeyGen). Conflating them is the first mistake buyers make, so the teardown below keeps them in separate lanes.

Teardown 1: the two lanes don't share a price logic

Generative tools sell credits; avatar tools sell seats and minutes. That difference breaks naive comparisons:

  • Runway / Pika / Luma (~$8–$12/mo): you buy a pool of credits and spend them per generation. A short uses a slice; a long, high-res render uses more. The real question is "how many usable clips does the pool yield," which depends on length and model — check the credit cost per generation type on the pricing page before committing.
  • Synthesia / HeyGen (~$22–$24/mo): you're renting an avatar presenter billed per seat, with a monthly minutes allowance. The cheaper plan looks similar in price to Runway, but it's a different asset: a talking spokesperson, not b-roll.

So the first number to pin down isn't the price — it's which lane your content is in.

Teardown 2: a real posting schedule

Take a solo creator posting two 30-second shorts a week (about 8–9 minutes of finished video a month):

  • Generative lane: If you generate several candidate clips per final short, you'll burn through a $12 Runway or $10 Luma pool fast at higher resolutions. Pika at $8 is the cheapest entry but expect to up-tier as volume grows. Budget realistically ~$12–$30/mo once you care about quality.
  • Avatar lane: Two scripted spokesperson clips a week fits inside a single ~$22–$24 seat with its minutes allowance — often comfortably. If your whole channel is talking-head explainers, the avatar seat is the better per-video math than generating scenes.

The crossover: the moment you need a human presenter, the avatar tools win on effort; the moment you need original cinematic b-roll, the generative tools win on flexibility. They aren't rivals so much as neighbors.

Teardown 3: the "unlimited" mirage

A few vendors dangle higher tiers with far more credits or "priority" generation. Read what "unlimited" excludes: queue priority, max resolution, watermark removal, and commercial rights are usually tier-gated. Runway's top tier (~$76/mo) removes caps that strangle the $12 plan; that 6× jump is the real price of uninterrupted work, not a rip-off — just know it exists before you hit the wall on the cheap plan.

Choose by your content, not the demo

  • You make original scenes / b-roll / motion from text or images: Runway for polish and tooling, Pika or Luma for a cheaper pool to start.
  • You film yourself but want a multilingual spokesperson: HeyGen's avatar + translation fits training and global content.
  • You ship corporate/educational explainers at volume: Synthesia's presenter model scales better than filming a human.
  • You want templates + stock + AI in one: InVideo AI lowers the edit floor if you don't live in an editor.

Frequently asked questions

Can an AI clone my face for video?

Yes, via consent-based avatar tools like HeyGen, where you record a short consent clip and the tool generates you speaking other scripts. It's intended for training, localization, and accessibility — not deception. Use it only with explicit consent and disclosure.

Which is cheapest to just try?

Pika (~$8/mo) and Luma (~$10/mo) are the lowest gates into generative video; Synthesia and HeyGen both sit near ~$22–$24/mo for a real avatar seat. Free tiers exist but cap resolution and watermark output.

Do these replace a video editor?

For first drafts and simple pieces, yes. For a finished, on-brand video with cuts, sound design, and correction, you'll still open an editor. Treat AI video as a fast first pass, not the whole post pipeline.

Are AI videos safe for commercial client work?

Check the license per tier. Entry plans sometimes restrict commercial use or resolution; paid tiers generally clear client work but may cap it. Confirm on the vendor's licensing page before billing a client.

The demos oversell effortlessness; the math is what keeps a channel running.

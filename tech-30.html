SaaS, Explained From the Billing Meter Up

Monday, 9:04 a.m., a library computer

Your laptop died on the train. You sit down at a public terminal, open a browser, sign in, and your entire business is there — email, documents, the CRM, the roadmap. Nothing is installed. That moment is the whole product. Software as a Service is the delivery model that makes "your software lives on someone else's computer and you rent access" feel like nothing happened at all.

Under the hood: one app, many tenants

The defining mechanism is multi-tenancy. A traditional install puts a copy of the software on your server. SaaS runs a single instance of the application on the vendor's infrastructure, and every customer's data is partitioned inside it — logically separate, physically shared. That's why the vendor can update everyone at once at 2 a.m. and why your "version" is never behind.

The trade-off is the core of the model: you trade control for someone else's operational burden. The vendor owns the servers, the patching, the backups, the scaling. You own a login and a monthly bill.

The billing meter

SaaS pricing is almost always a subscription per user per month, not a one-time license. The mechanism matters more than the marketing:

  • Google Workspace Business Starter lists at $6/user/month (workspace.google.com/pricing.html) — email, docs, drive, on Google's tenant.
  • Microsoft 365 Business Basic is about $6/user/month (microsoft.com/microsoft-365/business) — web apps plus email, on Microsoft's tenant.
  • Salesforce Sales Cloud starts around $25/user/month for the entry edition (salesforce.com/editions-pricing).

The "per user per month" meter is why SaaS scales cost with headcount — and why finance hates it when a $6 seat quietly becomes a $20 seat after three "minor" upgrades. The bill is the control surface; read it.

SLAs: the vendor's promise, written down

A Service Level Agreement is the contractual uptime commitment. Vendors typically promise "three nines" (99.9%) or "four nines" (99.99%) monthly availability, with service credits if they miss. The arithmetic is blunt: 99.9% allows about 43 minutes of downtime per month; 99.99% allows about 4 minutes. That number is the difference between "annoying" and "someone's on call." Cloud substrate providers publish their own SLAs — AWS, for instance, commits 99.99% for multi-AZ deployments of services like EC2 (aws.amazon.com/legal/service-level-agreements) — and SaaS vendors inherit and re-package that reliability.

Data residency and the fine print

Because your data sits on the vendor's infrastructure, where it sits is a real question. Major vendors let you choose a region (EU, US, etc.) to address GDPR and similar rules. The mechanism is straightforward: the partition holding your tenant is instantiated in a data center you select. If a vendor can't tell you where your data lives, that's a flag, not a footnote.

Why it won, and where it frays

The mechanical advantages are real: zero install, instant updates, elastic scaling, built-in backups. The fraying edges are equally mechanical:

  • Vendor lock-in. Your data is in their schema, their export format. Migration is a project, not a copy.
  • Cumulative cost. A $10/seat/mo tool across 50 people is $6,000/year — more than a one-time license would've been, indefinitely.
  • External dependency. When the vendor is down, you're down. Your SLA credit doesn't pay for your lost hour.

Frequently asked questions

Is SaaS the same as "cloud"?

No. "Cloud" is the infrastructure (servers you don't own). SaaS is one delivery model on top of it — software you rent rather than install. Cloud also covers IaaS (raw servers, e.g. AWS) and PaaS (platforms). SaaS is the most consumer-visible layer.

What happens to my data if I cancel?

Reputable vendors provide an export, but the format and completeness vary. That's the lock-in risk: read the export terms before you commit, not after. Data residency and portability should be part of your evaluation, not an afterthought.

Why is it always per-user pricing?

Because in multi-tenancy, each user consumes partitioned storage, compute, and support. Per-seat billing aligns the vendor's cost with your usage. It also means cost scales with headcount — predictable for them, cumulative for you.

How do I compare vendors fairly?

Compare total cost of ownership over three years, not the sticker price; check the SLA's actual downtime allowance; confirm data residency and export. A cheaper seat with weak export and a 99.5% SLA can cost more in a bad month than a pricier, more reliable one.

Verdict: SaaS is best understood as rented, multi-tenant software: one app instance, many logically-separated customers, billed per seat per month, with uptime backed by an SLA and data located in a region you can (usually) choose. It wins on zero-install and elastic scale, and frays on lock-in, cumulative cost, and shared-dependency risk. The caveat: the subscription meter never stops — evaluate the three-year total and the export terms before the first login, not after.

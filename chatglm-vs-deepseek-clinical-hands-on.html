ChatGLM 5.2 vs DeepSeek on a Real Breast Cancer Case — A Surgeon's Hands-on Test

By Tan Haosheng · 副主任医师 / 医学博士 (Associate Chief Physician, MD/PhD) · 甲状腺乳腺外科 (Thyroid & Breast Surgery), 泰州市人民医院 (Taizhou People's Hospital) · Last updated 2026-07-29
⚠️ Medical disclaimer / 医疗免责声明: All clinical case analyses, model evaluations, and treatment discussions on this site are for educational and research purposes only. They are based on a single anonymized case and do not constitute individual medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. For any real patient, always consult your own qualified physician and follow current guidelines (NCCN / CSCO / CBCS). AI outputs are not a substitute for professional clinical judgment. Last reviewed / 最后审阅: 2026-07-31.
🧪 原创实测 #1 / 5  ·  Hands-on Review 1 of 5
The question that started this: Can a free chatbot be trusted with a post-operative breast cancer treatment plan? I'm a thyroid & breast surgeon, not a prompt engineer — so instead of debating it, I ran the two cheapest models I could find on a real, fully anonymized case from my own specialty, and scored their answers against the 2026 Chinese Breast Cancer Society (CBCS) guideline with a fixed rubric. This is what happened. Spoiler: neither passed, but they failed in very different ways.

Why I ran this test

I work at a public hospital. My department's software budget does not stretch to GPT-4-class subscriptions, and I suspect most of my colleagues are in the same boat. What I do have is free web access to a handful of Chinese LLMs. So the practical question isn't "which model is theoretically best" — it's "which free model is still useful for the clinical-adjacent reasoning I actually do?"

I picked a task that is hard, checkable, and high-stakes: reading a post-op pathology report and recommending next-step adjuvant therapy for an early breast cancer case. If a model can't handle this, it can't be a decision aid. If it handles it but can't show its work, I still can't trust it. Two free models went head-to-head:

  • DeepSeek — web "fast mode" (网页快速模式), chat.deepseek.com
  • ChatGLM 5.2 (智谱清言) — web version, chatglm.cn

Both are $0. That's the whole point — this is a budget test, not a flagship-model bake-off.

How I tested it (so you can reproduce it)

I built a fixed framework so the comparison is fair and re-runnable:

  • One case, one prompt. A single anonymized outpatient-record screenshot (case + 16-part task list in one image) was shown to both models. Note: this DeepSeek-vs-ChatGLM comparison originally stated text was used because DeepSeek's expert mode could not read images; per the author's correction, the actual input across the series — including this pair — was the screenshot image.
  • Graded against 2026 CBCS. A 16-item / 100-point rubric keyed to the 2026 CBCS guideline (Version 2026.1.0) — staging, surgery, radiotherapy, chemo, endocrine therapy, safety, follow-up, and self-assessment.
  • 3 runs each, take the median. If the spread between best and worst run was ≥8 points, the model is flagged "unstable."
  • The citation rule. A correct answer that did not cite a specific 2026 CBCS chapter/page was capped at 1 point (out of 2). If I can't verify it actually read the guideline, I won't credit it for having done so.

You can copy the exact prompt and rubric from the open testing framework and run it yourself. That's the whole point of publishing this.

The case (anonymized)

A 60-year-old woman, post-op day 1 after right breast cancer surgery. Pathology: a dominant lesion with three foci of stromal invasion, largest focus 0.2 cm (2 mm), ER 90%+, PR 90%+, HER2 0, Ki-67 5%. Sentinel nodes negative (0/2 + 1/1). Discharge diagnosis: TisN0M0 (stage 0, DCIS).

The trap: "3 foci of invasion, largest 0.2 cm" should set off an alarm. Microinvasion is defined as a single focus ≤1 mm. A 2 mm focus already exceeds that → at minimum pT1a (Stage IA), not stage 0. Any model that just rubber-stamps "TisN0M0" is missing the single most clinically important discrepancy in the record. The guideline reference is CBCS 2026, TNM staging pp. 33–34, DCIS definition p. 50.

Bottom line: DeepSeek scored 5–6× higher and was rock-stable; ChatGLM was both low and erratic, and — bizarrely — answered a fully Chinese prompt in English. But "DeepSeek won" hides the more important story: both models failed the same core clinical reasoning, just at different depths.

Where DeepSeek failed (3 systematic misses — every run)

① Never challenged the staging (1.1 + 1.2, 16 pts lost). All three runs accepted "TisN0M0, stage 0" verbatim, despite the pathology explicitly describing 2 mm of invasion. Staging error is a safety-grade mistake here: stage 0 vs IA changes adjuvant intensity, follow-up rhythm, and the patient's whole risk narrative.
② Didn't downgrade endocrine therapy for post-mastectomy DCIS (3.2a, 12 pts lost). CBCS 2026 p.59 note a states that for DCIS after mastectomy, endocrine drugs fall under chemoprevention — "consider / optional," not a strong recommendation. DeepSeek applied the "DCIS → endocrine is strongly recommended" template without distinguishing mastectomy from breast-conserving surgery. This was the highest-discrimination item in the rubric; it scored zero.
③ Didn't cite the AI-vs-TAM age cutoff (3.2b, 10 pts lost). CBCS 2026 p.59 note b: AI's advantage over tamoxifen is mainly in post-menopausal women <60; at ≥60 the two are near-equivalent. The patient is exactly 60 — right on the boundary. DeepSeek just said "AI first" without showing it knew the cutoff existed.

To its credit, DeepSeek correctly waived chemotherapy (one run even mentioned Oncotype DX), gave reasonable follow-up frequency, and flagged bone-density/Vit-D monitoring. And its three runs were identical — meaning the output is templated, not random, so you can at least predict what you'll get.

Where ChatGLM failed (2 independent flaws)

① Answered entirely in English (usability dealbreaker). The prompt and the medical record were both Chinese; all three ChatGLM runs replied in English. For a Chinese clinician that means higher re-reading cost, lost nuance in guideline terminology and dosing, and effectively — handing the decision to a model that "didn't think in your language." Unacceptable in a clinical context regardless of score.
② Extremely short and vague, and unstable (median 5, spread 8). Average ~130 characters per run (DeepSeek was ~450). Mostly "here's what the record says + generic advice + disclaimer." Run 2 scored just 2/100 — almost a restatement of the case plus "let the doctor decide." The spread of 8 trips the framework's "unstable" threshold, so the same question repeated can return wildly different quality.

Notably, both models failed the same three core items (staging challenge, DCIS-endocrine downgrade, age cutoff) and both scored 0/16 on citing the guideline. The difference is depth: DeepSeek gave a "wrong but checkable" reasoning chain; ChatGLM gave "vague and uncheckable."

The verdict: which model for which task

DeepSeek fast mode ≫ ChatGLM 5.2 on Chinese clinical reasoning — it wins on every comparable axis, and it's in Chinese so I can actually verify it. But the real conclusion is narrower and more useful than "DeepSeek is better": neither free model is safe to trust alone on staging or nuanced guideline interpretation. Both are confidently wrong on exactly the items where a junior doctor would most need a reliable second opinion.

What I actually do with these tools

First person, no hedging: I use free LLMs as drafting and second-opinion aids, never as decision authority. They're genuinely good at the boring 80% — a clean discharge-summary draft at 11pm, an English polish on a manuscript, a patient-explanation script. The moment the task becomes "interpret this pathology and pick the therapy," I read the model's output the way I'd read a junior colleague's note: check the staging, check the recommendation grade, and never accept a confident assertion I can't trace to the guideline.

The single most important lesson from this test isn't "DeepSeek beat ChatGLM." It's the 0/16 citation rate. Free models paraphrase guidelines; they don't quote them. That's fine for drafting and dangerous for deciding.

Limitations

  • One case, image (screenshot of the outpatient record) input, free tiers only, three runs per model.
  • I did not test DeepSeek's deeper "reasoning" mode (if offered) or any paid API — that's a candidate for a later review in this series.
  • Scoring is one surgeon's interpretation of a fixed rubric; the framework is published so you can disagree and re-score.

Frequently asked questions

Did you test the paid versions of these models?

No. This is deliberately a budget test of the free web tiers. DeepSeek's deeper reasoning mode and any paid API tier are left for a later review in this 5-part series.

Why score 0 on guideline citations for both?

The rubric capped a correct-but-uncited answer at 1 point and required a specific 2026 CBCS chapter/page for full credit. Neither model ever cited a chapter or page — they said "mainstream / authoritative / latest guideline" without specifics, so I could not verify they had actually read the source.

Can I reproduce this myself?

Yes. The full prompt (case + 16-item rubric) is published at /clinical-benchmark/framework.html as a transcription of the screenshot. In the real test the model received the image; paste the text version into any model to reproduce the scoring against the 2026 CBCS guideline.

Is ChatGLM usable for anything clinical then?

Its English output is a genuine asset for reading international literature, but for Chinese clinical decision support it answered in English and was both vague and unstable — not something I'd rely on. DeepSeek fast mode is the better free pick for Chinese-language reasoning, with the caveats above.

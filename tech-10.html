The claim: Android owns the planet, iOS owns the revenue

Roughly 70% of the world's smartphones run Android; iOS holds the rest (StatCounter, global mobile share). But if you ship software for a living, the split that pays is inverted: iOS users spend more per capita, so a small install base still drives outsized app revenue. That tension — reach vs. monetization — is the whole comparison in one line. Everything below is the engineering detail behind it.

From where I sit as a practitioner: if you need maximum reach on a bootstrap budget, Android is unavoidable. If you need per-user value, iOS is where the money is.

Log: UI and app model

iOS favors consistency — one navigation grammar across devices, sandboxed apps, uniform system APIs. That uniformity is why a build "just works" across iPhones. Android gives you launchers, default-app choice, and widget freedom, at the cost of fragmentation: your layout must survive a thousand screen sizes and OEM skins. For a small team, iOS is less testing surface; Android is more reach.

Log: security architecture

Both ship a modern baseline — sandboxed apps, verified boot, regular patches. The differing philosophy:

  • iOS: walled garden. App Review gatekeepers, App Tracking Transparency requiring explicit ad-tracking consent, hardware-backed keys in the Secure Enclave. Privacy is enforced at the platform level.
  • Android: open by design. Google Play Protect scans sideloaded and store apps; granular runtime permissions; but the larger attack surface (sideloading, varied OEM builds) shifts more responsibility to the user.

Public reporting consistently shows iOS with a slight edge in privacy defaults; Android with more user control. Neither is "unsafe" — both patch known CVEs, and the bigger risk is an unupdated device on either side.

The practitioner takeaway: an iPhone or a Pixel ages predictably; a random Android bargain may stop receiving patches within a year. For a device you'll keep three years, update policy is a spec, not a footnote.

Log: customization and control

Android wins the flexibility benchmark outright — alternate launchers, sideloading, even root on some builds. iOS has added widgets and an app library but keeps the gates. If "control over my own device" is the priority, Android; if "it works and stays safe without my attention" is the priority, iOS.

Pick by the job

  • You're a developer maximizing reach: Android first, iOS for revenue.
  • You want set-and-forget security: recent iPhone or Pixel.
  • You want to tinker: Android, preferably a line with long support.
  • You live in one vendor's ecosystem (Apple/Microsoft/Google): match it.

Frequently asked questions

Which is more secure?

Both are secure by modern standards. iOS enforces privacy at the platform level; Android gives more control and a larger surface. An updated device on either is far safer than an unupdated one on either.

Do I get more apps on one?

Both stores host millions of apps. iOS often gets new features first due to device uniformity; Android's open model yields more free and varied apps. The gap that mattered in 2015 has largely closed.

Which lasts longer?

Recent iPhones and Pixels lead on update years (5–7). Cheaper Android lines can lag. Check the specific model's support promise before buying.

Can I switch later?

Yes, but expect friction: messages, photos, and app data don't all transfer cleanly. Decide within the first year if you can, or budget a migration afternoon.

Verdict: There is no winner, only a fit. Android's ~70% footprint makes it the reach play; iOS's monetization and set-and-forget security make it the safe-and-profitable play. For a practitioner, the real decision is update policy and ecosystem lock-in, not the logo. The honest caveat: any comparison ages fast as both platforms borrow each other's features — re-check the support window for the exact model you buy.

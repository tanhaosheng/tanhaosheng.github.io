We've been promised VR before

VR is the field's own ghost. In 1968 Ivan Sutherland strapped on the first head-mounted display; in 1995 Nintendo shipped the Virtual Boy and the public laughed; through the 2010s every January show declared "this is the year." I've been in headsets across all of it, and the 2026 version is the first that's genuinely good rather than promising. That history matters, because it inoculates you against the two current extremes: the hype that VR replaces your monitor, and the cynicism that it's still a toy.

Myth 1: "VR is only for gaming"

Gaming is the visible edge, but the same headsets run training sims, design reviews, and fitness apps. The reason gaming leads is simple: it funded the hardware. Don't buy a headset expecting to replace your workstation — buy it for presence, then be surprised by the rest.

Myth 2: "You need a $3,499 headset"

The Apple Vision Pro sits at the top as a spatial-computing device, not a gaming box. For actual VR play, the mainstream standalone headset (Meta's Quest line) anchors around $499 — no PC required. Spending more gets you a different category, not a better game.

Myth 3: "VR isolates you"

The strongest social experiences in VR — shared worlds, co-op rhythm games, remote presence — are more connecting than a voice call. The isolation claim was true of early single-player demos; it isn't true of the live services people actually use in 2026.

Myth 4: "It makes everyone sick"

Comfort has improved dramatically with higher refresh rates, better lenses, and rendered-world techniques that reduce latency. Some people are still sensitive, especially to artificial locomotion. The honest advice from experienced users: start with seated/room-scale experiences, use comfort settings, and build tolerance. It's a real constraint, not a dealbreaker for most.

Myth 5: "It's a passing fad"

The install base and software libraries have compounded for several years now. Fad implies a spike and collapse; VR has had the collapse (1995, 2016 hype) and kept climbing. Whether it goes mainstream is still open — but "fad" is the wrong word.

What actually matters when buying

  • Library fit: If your friends are on one platform, that's your platform. VR is social.
  • Comfort and weight: You'll wear it for an hour; spec sheets don't capture fit.
  • Space: Room-scale needs roughly 2m×2m clear. If you can't clear it, a seated standalone still works.
  • PC or not: Standalone is simpler; tethered unlocks higher-fidelity PC VR if you already own the machine.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a gaming PC for VR in 2026?

Not for standalone headsets like the Quest line — they run games on-device. A PC unlocks higher-fidelity PC VR (and PSVR2 needs a PlayStation). Buy standalone unless you specifically want PC-VR titles.

Is the Vision Pro worth it for gaming?

For pure gaming, no — its library is thin and its price is for spatial computing and media. If gaming is the goal, a Quest or PSVR2 is the better spend.

How much space do I need?

Room-scale works best with about 2m×2m clear of obstacles. Seated and stationary experiences need far less. Clear cables and tripping hazards regardless.

Will 2026 headsets be much better than current ones?

Expect incremental gains — brighter panels, lighter bodies, better passthrough — not a category break. If you have a working current-gen headset, waiting for a marginal refresh isn't urgent.

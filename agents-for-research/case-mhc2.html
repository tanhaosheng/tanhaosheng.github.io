Case 2 — MHC-II / NK co-culture: the cross-tumor trap

By Tan Haosheng, MD, PhD · Last reviewed 2026-07-31

Trap test~4 min wall-clockSame scRNA-seq data as Case 1Different paper, different tumor typeWorkBuddy Hy3Reproducible transcript

The single-cell dataset is the same breast-cancer samples from Case 1. The paper is different on purpose. It's an NK co-culture study on head-and-neck squamous cell carcinoma and thyroid cancer — co-culture with NK cells, MHC-II upregulation, LAG-3 inhibition. Wrong tumor. Wrong immune-pressure design. Wrong everything to validate against breast-cancer scRNA-seq. I gave the agent the same data and this second paper, with one short follow-up prompt. It answered in about 4 minutes — faster than Case 1 because the dataset was already parsed in the same session, so this run measures reasoning and cross-checking, not data loading. This case tests failure mode ④ — domain-mismatch blindness.

⚠️ About the numbers and figures on this page

The single-cell dataset behind this case supports a manuscript that has not been submitted yet. I am not free to publish the underlying values, and I am not going to pretend otherwise.

So: every figure below was redrawn from the real analysis with the values deliberately perturbed, and the numbers quoted in the text are rounded to low precision. What is preserved is the direction, ranking and rough magnitude of each effect — which is the part that matters for judging what the AI agent did. What is not preserved is any exact value.

Read the charts as illustrations of a pattern, not as data. Once the manuscript is published I will swap in the unmodified figures and link the real tables.

The prompt I gave the agent (verbatim)

10-MHC-II.docx 这篇文章的结论支持吗？

That's the entire second prompt. The first session had already loaded the breast-cancer data and the KLRB1 manuscript. I just dropped the MHC-II paper in and asked: does this one's conclusions get supported?

Hard limits the agent named before any conclusion

This is the part that matters most. Before running any analysis, the agent flagged two structural mismatches that would have invalidated any quantitative comparison:

Two constraints the agent named up front

  1. Wrong cancer type. The dataset is breast cancer (luminal / mixed microenvironment); the paper is HNSCC + thyroid. Tumor-cell MHC-II regulation and immune-compartment composition differ substantially across these sites. Any quantitative number from one does not transfer to the other.
  2. No NK pressure pairing. The paper's mechanism depends on NK co-culture inducing MHC-II upregulation. The dataset has only two conditions (CD45-sorted / unsorted), not an NK-pressure arm. The dataset cannot test the central mechanistic claim, full stop.

The agent still went on to evaluate what could be evaluated cross-tumor — but with these caveats attached to every conclusion. That's the difference between a usable output and a fabricated one.

Result 1 — LAG-3 enrichment in cytotoxic T/NK

This part the agent could evaluate cross-tumor, because the LAG-3 / cytotoxic axis is not strongly tumor-type-specific.

✓ LAG-3⁺ cells have ~2.3× the cytotoxic score of LAG-3⁻ cells

LAG3⁺ cells carry roughly 2.3× the cytotoxic score of LAG3⁻ cells. The gap survives depth correction (odds ratio a little above 2) and the p-value is far past any reasonable threshold — this is not a marginal effect.

Every individual effector moves the same way, and the strong ones move hard: GZMB and NKG7 both around a 4-fold enrichment, CD8A close behind, GNLY around 2.5-fold.

Sub-compartment distribution of LAG3⁺ cells: CD8⁺ effector/exhausted T 17.9% > proliferating T 10.7% ≈ NKT-like γδ T 10.2% >> CD4⁺ memory T 5.9% > Treg 2.2%. Same ranking pattern the paper reports.

△ But the effect-size correlation is much weaker than the paper's

Within cell type, though, the continuous LAG3-versus-cytotoxicity correlation is weak — roughly a third of what the paper reports in its own dataset. The agent noted that the 2.3× ratio matches in direction but the continuous correlation magnitude is much smaller in this breast-cancer data — consistent with the cross-tumor caveat, but also consistent with sample-size and depth-distribution differences. The agent did not claim this is a refutation.

Result 2 — Tumor-cell MHC-II expression

✓ Epithelial tumor cells do express MHC-II, and MHC-I is intact

MHC-II is detectable on tumour epithelium — HLA-DRA in roughly half of epithelial cells, CD74 in about two thirds.

MHC-I is clearly intact — HLA-B, HLA-C and B2M are all positive in the large majority of epithelial cells, consistent with the paper's claim that MHC-I is not downregulated in this setting.

But myeloid cells are essentially all MHC-II positive, and their mean MHC-II expression is close to an order of magnitude above the tumour epithelium. The agent flagged this asymmetry explicitly rather than letting the epithelial signal stand on its own.

✗ MHC-II gene co-linearity is much weaker than the paper reports

The paper claims tight co-expression among MHC-II genes. In this breast-cancer data the intra-epithelial correlation is roughly one fifth of that. Only inside myeloid cells does it climb toward the paper's range, and even there it stays below it.

The agent's interpretation: tight MHC-II co-linearity is a myeloid signature, not a tumor-epithelial signature. The paper's claim, if validated in HNSCC + thyroid, may not generalize to epithelial cells in breast cancer.

✗ STAT1 / CIITA drive MHC-II — correlation is far weaker here

The paper reports a moderate STAT1 ↔ MHC-II correlation plus CIITA involvement. In the breast-cancer data, STAT1's best correlation with any MHC-II component is under half the paper's figure, and CIITA — the canonical MHC-II master regulator — is close to nothing. Both fall well short.

Writing recommendations

The agent's evaluation ended with three explicit writing recommendations, which I am following as I revise the MHC-II draft:

  1. Cite cross-tumor for LAG-3 enrichment only. The 2.3× ratio and the sub-compartment ranking of LAG3⁺ cells are robust enough to cite this breast-cancer data as supportive. The continuous ρ does not transfer, so do not put a specific number in the cross-tumor discussion.
  2. Do not cite this dataset for MHC-II co-linearity or STAT1/CIITA correlations. Including those numbers in a cross-tumor paragraph would actively weaken the paper's quantitative claims. Keep the paper's own bulk / co-culture data as the source.
  3. Add a "limits of cross-tumor inference" annotation. When the paper's discussion mentions MHC-II regulation in epithelial cells, it should explicitly note that this breast-cancer dataset shows different magnitudes and that the mechanism may be tumor-site-specific.

Additional methodological flag from the agent

The HLA-DRA-high epithelial cells in this dataset show a small but consistent enrichment for LYZ⁺ / C1QA⁺ signal — a myeloid marker pattern. The agent flagged this as a possible ambient-RNA contamination artifact: free RNA from myeloid cells in the input solution gets attributed to tumor cells in the count matrix. Before the paper cites any epithelial MHC-II percentage from this dataset, it should run a decontamination step (e.g. SoupX, DecontX) and re-measure.

What this case taught me about AI agents

  • The most useful behavior was refusal to fully answer. A weaker agent would have said "yes the data supports your paper" with a confident paragraph. This one said "here is what I can evaluate cross-tumor, here is what I cannot, here is what the numbers actually say." That's the difference between a tool I can cite and a tool I cannot.
  • Mismatch-naming has to happen early, not late. The agent named "wrong cancer type" and "no NK pressure pairing" in the first paragraph of its reply, before any number. That ordering matters — once an agent has shown you numbers, you tend to trust them; the structural caveat has to come first.
  • Cross-tumor inference is not a binary yes/no. Some claims transfer (LAG-3 enrichment in cytotoxic T/NK is broadly conserved). Others don't (MHC-II co-linearity and STAT1/CIITA correlations are tumor-site-dependent). A useful agent splits these; a fabrication-prone one treats the paper as a single yes/no target.
  • Ambient RNA contamination is a real risk in single-cell tumor data. An agent that just reports "% of epithelial cells expressing HLA-DRA" without a contamination check is selling you numbers that may not exist.

Limitations of this case

  • Cross-tumor inference is the test — that is also the case's limit. The MHC-II paper's HNSCC + thyroid mechanism is not directly validated or refuted by breast-cancer data.
  • One patient, two samples. No claim about cohort-level behavior.
  • Ambient-RNA decontamination was recommended but not run inside this session; the reported HLA-DRA percentage on tumor epithelium is the pre-decontamination number.

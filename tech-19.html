The claim: "free" VPNs are the most expensive privacy tool you'll own

Let's start with the uncomfortable truth the ads hide. A VPN that charges $0 has to make money — and the product is usually you. Multiple free services have been caught logging and selling user activity. So the real choice isn't free vs. paid; it's "pay with money or pay with your data." Below, the two sides of the argument, then the math.

How a VPN actually works (30 seconds)

Your traffic is encrypted and tunneled through a server the provider runs. Sites see the VPN's IP, not yours. Your ISP can't read the contents. That's the whole mechanism. What it does not do is make you anonymous — cookies, logins, and browser fingerprinting survive the tunnel.

The duel: for vs. against

Both columns are true. The provocateur's read: most people overbuy the fantasy ("total anonymity") and underbuy the reality ("safe on cafe WiFi"). Pick the column that matches your actual problem.

What the experts actually say

The Electronic Frontier Foundation has repeatedly framed a VPN as one privacy layer among many, not a silver bullet — it won't hide you from a site you log into, and it won't stop trackers that ride your identity. Independent security audits (published by the better providers) are the only real evidence a "no-logs" claim holds. Translation: pay for an audited provider, or don't bother.

Real-world scenarios

  • Cafe WiFi, quick email: a VPN (or just use HTTPS) — low stakes, easy win.
  • Activist/journalist in a restrictive region: a vetted, audited VPN is closer to essential.
  • "I want to disappear from the internet": a VPN alone won't do it. Combine with the controls in our data-privacy guide.
  • Streaming a service you already pay for while traveling: works sometimes, blocked often — don't buy a VPN assuming it always will.

Frequently asked questions

Are free VPNs safe?

Some (Proton's free tier) are run by reputable companies. Many are not — logging and resale are documented. If the product is free, you are likely the product. Prefer a known audited provider's free tier over an unknown one.

Which protocol should I use?

WireGuard is the modern default — fast and lean. OpenVPN remains solid and configurable. Avoid PPTP and L2TP; both have known weaknesses. Most premium apps default to WireGuard now.

Does a VPN make me anonymous?

No. It hides your IP from sites and your traffic from your ISP, but accounts, cookies, and fingerprints still identify you. Treat it as a privacy layer, not a mask.

Do I need one at home?

Usually not. On your own trusted network with HTTPS everywhere, the marginal privacy gain is small. The stronger case is travel, public WiFi, and regions with network restrictions.

Skip the free logger, skip the "total anonymity" fantasy. The honest caveat: a VPN is one layer in a privacy stack, not the stack.

Eleven nines, and the bill that ignores them

Amazon advertises S3 durability at 99.999999999% — "eleven nines." That's a real, documented claim about the probability of losing an object in a year (aws.amazon.com/s3/storage-classes). It's an impressive number and almost beside the point for most newcomers, because the thing that actually injures people on AWS isn't data loss — it's the bill. The myths around that bill are worth killing first.

Myth 1: "You only pay for what you use"

True in spirit, misleading in practice. Compute is pay-per-hour. But the meter also runs on things you didn't intend to buy: data egress (traffic leaving AWS), NAT gateway processing, load balancer hours, and idle EBS volumes left attached to a stopped instance. Community reports are full of "$5 experiment, $80 month" stories where the surprise was a forgotten load balancer or cross-region replication. The honest version: you pay for what you provision and what moves, and "what moves" is easy to overlook.

Myth 2: "The cloud is cheaper than a server"

Only if you're elastic. A server you run 24/7 on EC2 often costs more over three years than buying hardware. AWS wins when your load spikes and shrinks; it loses when your load is flat and permanent. Reserved Instances (1- or 3-year commitments) and Spot Instances (spare capacity at up to ~90% discount, per AWS) close the gap — but they're choices you have to make, not defaults (aws.amazon.com/ec2/pricing).

Myth 3: "No ops required"

Managed services reduce ops; they don't delete them. Someone still configures IAM, sets budgets, rotates keys, and reads the bill. The "we moved to cloud and fired the sysadmin" story usually ends with a contractor re-hired at 2x to clean up the bill.

What each core service actually is

EC2 — a rented virtual server. You pick an instance type (CPU/RAM profile) and an OS image, and you pay by the hour or second. The free tier gives new accounts 750 hours/month of t3.micro for 12 months — enough to run one small server continuously at no cost (aws.amazon.com/free). The catch is that "one server" quietly becomes "one server + a load balancer + a NAT gateway + logs," and only the server was in your mental budget.

S3 — object storage with a durability obsession. You put objects in "buckets" and pay per gigabyte per month, plus a little per request. The 11-nines durability figure means AWS designs against hardware failure, not against you deleting a file or writing a public-read policy by mistake. The famous S3 disasters are almost always permission errors, not AWS losing data. Treat the bucket policy as the real security boundary.

Lambda — code that runs on events, not servers. You upload a function; AWS runs it when triggered (an HTTP request, a file upload, a timer). You pay per invocation and per compute-second, with a generous free tier. The win is zero idle cost — a function that runs 100 times a day costs almost nothing. The limit is that long-running or tightly-coupled workflows fight the model; at some scale a plain EC2 box is cheaper and simpler.

Where the money actually goes (scenarios)

  • A personal portfolio site: S3 + CloudFront. A few cents a month, or free inside the free tier. AWS is frankly overkill, but the bill is trivial.
  • A startup with a 10-person dev team: EC2 + RDS + S3 + Lambda for glue. Realistic monthly bill lands in the low hundreds once you leave the free tier — dominated by RDS and EC2, not by storage.
  • A data pipeline moving terabytes out to the internet: Egress becomes the line item that surprises you. This is where a careful architecture (region choice, CloudFront caching, compression) pays for itself.

Frequently asked questions

Is the AWS Free Tier really free?

For 12 months on specific services (750 hrs/mo of t3.micro EC2, 5GB of S3, etc.), yes — as long as you stay within the quotas. Exceed them, or use services not in the free tier, and you're billed. Set a billing alarm on day one; the free tier has no mercy for forgetting.

Why is my bill higher than my compute?

Egress, NAT gateway, load balancers, and idle EBS volumes are the usual culprits. Pull the cost-by-service breakdown in the billing console and sort — the top three line items are rarely what you expected.

EC2 or Lambda for a new project?

If the workload is a steady server with persistent state, EC2 (or a container service). If it's event-driven, bursty, and stateless, Lambda's zero-idle-cost model usually wins. Many real systems use both — Lambda for glue, EC2 for the long-lived core.

How do I avoid being locked in?

AWS services are sticky by design. Mitigate with infrastructure-as-code (Terraform), portable data formats, and avoiding the most proprietary managed features unless they earn their keep. Full portability isn't free, but a clean IaC repo makes exit a project rather than a rewrite.

Verdict: AWS is not a product you "buy" — it's a meter you attach to a architecture. EC2 for servers, S3 for objects, Lambda for event-driven glue, RDS for managed SQL. The caveat: the durability headline (11 nines) is real but irrelevant next to the real risks — forgotten idle resources, egress, and permission mistakes. Read the cost-by-service breakdown monthly, set a billing alarm on day one, and remember the cloud saves money only when your load is elastic or your commitments are deliberate.

Friday night, the call freezes

The video call stutters. Someone blames the internet. It's almost never the internet — it's the Wi-Fi between the router and the laptop, two rooms and a fridge away. Router choice is the difference between "fine" and "why is this so bad," and most buying advice is a list of spec numbers nobody will feel. Here is a checklist that maps the box to the problem.

Ask yourself first

1. How big is the space, and what's in the walls? Concrete and metal kill signal; drywall doesn't. A 600 sq ft apartment and a 2,500 sq ft house need different answers.

2. How many devices, really? A home in 2026 easily has 30+ clients — phones, laptops, TVs, speakers, cameras. Cheap routers choke on client count before they choke on speed.

3. Do you own any Wi-Fi 6E or 7 device? If not, you're paying for a 6 GHz band no device can use. Wi-Fi 6 (no E) is still the sensible buy for most.

4. Can you run one cable? A wired backhaul between mesh nodes beats wireless backhaul every time. If you can't cable, halve your expectations.

5. Do you want control or calm? Ubiquiti and Asus reward tweakers; Eero and Nest reward people who never want to open a settings page.

The "up to" lie, briefly

A router advertised at "up to 5.4 Gbps" is quoting the theoretical PHY rate under ideal conditions with the right client. Real sustained throughput to one device is typically a fraction of that, and it drops with distance and interference. The number sells the box; it does not describe your living room. Judge by client capacity, bands, and your own square footage.

The per-option read

Asus gives you the most knobs — QoS, VLANs, custom firmware paths. The "gaming" label is mostly marketing, but the underlying QoS and client handling are genuinely strong for power users. Buy it if you'll actually configure it.

Eero is the calm choice: plug in, it works, it self-heals. You trade granular control for never thinking about it. TrueMesh does the placement math for you.

Netgear Orbi targets big homes with dedicated backhaul radios, so coverage holds up across distance. You pay for the radios and the pack.

TP-Link Deco is the value mesh — competent coverage at a fraction of Orbi's price. The trade is fewer high-end features and a lighter app.

Ubiquiti / UniFi is for people who want a real network console: VLANs, per-device limits, observability. Steeper setup, best long-term control.

Google Nest Wifi is the friendly default — fine coverage, simple app, Google-assistant nodes if you want them. Good until you outgrow "simple."

Scenarios, mapped

  • Apartment, <800 sq ft: one good Wi-Fi 6 router (Asus or even Nest single). Mesh is overkill.
  • House with dead spots: a 2–3 node mesh (Eero, Deco, Orbi). Cable the nodes if you can.
  • Gamer: wire the PC with Ethernet — that beats any "gaming router." Use Asus QoS only if wireless is unavoidable.
  • Tinkerer / home lab: Ubiquiti or Asus.
  • Set-and-forget family: Eero or Nest.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need Wi-Fi 6E?

Only if you already own 6E/7 devices that can use the 6 GHz band. Otherwise Wi-Fi 6 is the sane buy, and the "up to" number on the box won't change your experience.

Is a "gaming router" worth it?

Rarely. The useful part — QoS and strong client handling — exists on non-gaming Asus units too. The RGB and the word "gaming" are the premium. Wire your PC instead; that's the real win.

Will mesh slow me down?

Wireless backhaul cuts throughput versus a cable; wired backhaul doesn't. If you can run one Ethernet between nodes, mesh performs close to a wired AP.

How many nodes do I need?

One per major coverage zone, with overlap. A 2,000 sq ft home usually needs two; concrete walls add a node. Placement beats count — center them, not in corners.

Verdict: Most people over-buy the speed number and under-buy coverage and client capacity. Match the box to square footage and device count: a single Wi-Fi 6 router for small spaces, a wired-backhaul mesh for homes with dead zones, Asus or Ubiquiti for control, Eero or Nest for calm. The caveat: no router fixes a Wi-Fi problem that's actually a "run a cable" problem — and the "up to" gigabit figure will not be your living-room number.

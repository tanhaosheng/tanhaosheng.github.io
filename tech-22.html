The night 32 million passwords leaked

In 2009, RockYou — a then-popular widget maker — was breached and 32 million passwords were published in plaintext. No 2FA in the world helps if the server stores your password unhashed. But the broader lesson stuck: passwords alone fail, repeatedly, to phishing, reuse, and database theft. The fix the industry landed on is "something you know plus something you have." The catch is that not all "something you have" is equal, and most advice stops one sentence too early.

A skeptic's read on the famous stat

You'll see "accounts with 2FA are 99% less likely to be compromised." That figure traces to Microsoft's 2019 statement that MFA blocks 99.9% of automated attacks. Read it precisely: it holds against automated bot attacks, not against a targeted attacker who already has your session or who phishes the second factor live. 2FA is necessary and not sufficient. Treat it as raising the cost of attack, not as a wall.

Best method, by who you are

Casual personal user. An authenticator app (2FAS, Authy, or Google Authenticator) beats SMS at zero cost. The reported failure mode: people set it up, then lose the phone and have no backup codes. Fix before it happens — save the backup codes now.

Someone who loses phones. A hardware key as primary plus the authenticator as backup. A single YubiKey 5 starts around $25; buy two so one is a spare. The cost is trivial next to the account it protects.

Developer / self-hoster. Hardware key via FIDO2 for your accounts, and offer WebAuthn sign-in in anything you build. Passkeys are now supported across major platforms, so the "something you have" is often just your phone or laptop.

Journalist / activist at risk. Hardware key primary, authenticator encrypted backup, printed codes in a safe place. Origin-binding matters most here because targeted phishing is the real threat, not bots.

Small business. Don't trust users to choose well — enforce a policy. FIDO2/WebAuthn where supported, TOTP as fallback, SMS disabled. The spec that makes this enforceable is WebAuthn's origin-binding.

Enterprise. Roll out phishing-resistant FIDO2 and drop SMS as a fallback. This is the only tier where "99.9%" actually approaches "wall."

What users report going wrong

The dominant real-world failure is not hacking — it's self-lockout. No backup codes saved, phone dies, account unreachable. The second is "one method for everything": if your email's 2FA depends on that same email, you've built a loop. Diversify: hardware key primary, app backup, printed codes stored safely.

Frequently asked questions

Is SMS 2FA worthless?

No — it's far better than nothing and stops most automated attacks. But it's the weakest factor here: SIM-swapping and email takeover defeat it. Use it only as a last resort, never as primary.

Which authenticator app is best?

2FAS and Authy both offer encrypted backups; Google Authenticator now syncs too. Pick one that lets you export or back up, so a lost phone isn't a dead end. The codes themselves are standard TOTP either way.

Are passkeys replacing hardware keys?

They overlap. A passkey is FIDO2 that lives on your device and may sync via your account; a hardware key is a physical FIDO2 token. For highest-value accounts, a physical key remains the most robust "something you have."

What does "phishing-resistant" mean technically?

WebAuthn keys are bound to the origin (domain). A phishing site can't replay the signature because it isn't the real domain. That's why a hardware key or passkey survives a fake-login page that would steal a TOTP code.

Verdict: The right 2FA is role-dependent: authenticator app for casual users, hardware FIDO2 key for anyone high-value or at risk, passkeys where supported, and a enforced WebAuthn policy for teams. The caveat: the method only helps if you also saved backup codes and didn't tie your email's second factor to that email. 2FA raises the cost of attack; it does not end it.

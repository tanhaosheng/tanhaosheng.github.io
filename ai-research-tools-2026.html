Most "research tools" don't do research

Most "AI research tools" don't do research. They do retrieval. They find papers; they don't read them for you, weigh the methods, or tell you the study was underpowered. That gap eats your afternoon — and it's the single distinction that should drive your choice. Pick the tool by the job, not by the logo.

What each one is actually for

Perplexity is the fast first pass: ask a question, get an answer with clickable sources. Great for "what's the current consensus on X," weak for systematic literature review because it samples, not exhausts.

Consensus is built to answer questions from peer-reviewed literature and shows the studies behind a claim. If you want "what does the evidence say," this is closer to research than retrieval.

Elicit is the PDF-workhorse: point it at papers and it extracts findings, populations, and effect sizes into a table. For a lit review with 40 studies, this is where the time saves.

Scite is the citation-context tool: it shows whether papers cite a work in support or in contradiction. That one feature catches the "widely cited but actually disputed" trap better than any of the others.

Semantic Scholar and Google Scholar are the free backbone — search, alerting, citation graphs. No AI chat, but no paywall either, and every specialized tool above ultimately points back to them.

Real scenarios

  • You're a master's student scoping a thesis: Consensus to map the field, Google Scholar alerts to stay current, Scite to check a foundational paper isn't contested.
  • You're a clinician doing a quick evidence check: Consensus for the synthesis, then click through to the primary trials.
  • You're a PhD bottling 60 papers into a table: Elicit does the extraction; Scholar finds what Elicit misses.

Frequently asked questions

Can these replace reading the papers?

No. They summarize and retrieve; the judgment about methodology, sample size, and bias is still yours. Treat output as a map, not the territory.

Which is best for a systematic review?

Elicit for extraction and Consensus for synthesis, with Google Scholar as the search floor. None removes the need to read the included studies.

Are free tiers enough for students?

Often yes for light use. Pay when query limits or extraction caps actually block your work — not before.

Do they cover non-English literature?

Coverage is strongest for English. If your field publishes heavily in another language, Scholar's broader index still beats the AI layers. Check your language before committing.

Verdict: There is no single "best research tool" — only the one matched to the step you're on. Use Perplexity or Consensus to scope, Elicit to extract, Scite to sanity-check citations, and Scholar as the free floor beneath all of it. The caveat: every one of these summarizes other people's reading; none of them have done your research for you, and a contested citation still needs your eyes on the source.

I have a confession: I spent two weekends in 2025 telling people they could "just run a 70B model at home." They couldn't. I was repeating a slogan, not doing the arithmetic. The phrase "runs locally" has quietly become one of the most abused claims in the AI tool world — slapped on everything from a 1.5B model that fits in a browser tab to a 405B beast that needs a server rack and a power bill. This guide is the correction. No hype, just the physics and the math, so you can look at your own machine and know, before downloading a single gigabyte, what will actually work.

Everything below is compiled from public model cards (Llama, Qwen, DeepSeek), the llama.cpp and Ollama documentation, Apple's ML reporting on unified memory, and published quantization-retention studies. Where I give a number, it's a real specification or a derived calculation — not a vibe. I have not benchmarked every card myself; the bandwidth figures are manufacturer specs.

How this guide was built: Specifications and pricing are from official sources (NVIDIA, Apple, model card repos) as of mid-2026. VRAM estimates use the standard formula params × bits ÷ 8. Your mileage varies with driver, context length, and how many browser tabs you forgot to close.

The one equation that decides everything

Before any myth, one formula. A model's weight in gigabytes is:

model_size_GB ≈ parameters_in_billions × bits_per_parameter ÷ 8

A 7B model in 4-bit precision: 7 × 4 ÷ 8 = 3.5 GB. A 70B model in 4-bit: 70 × 4 ÷ 8 = 35 GB. The same model in 16-bit (the "full" precision weights): 70 × 16 ÷ 8 = 140 GB. That single multiplication is why your laptop refuses to load things. Most consumer laptops ship with 8–24 GB of video memory; the model needs to live in VRAM (or, on a Mac, unified memory) to run at usable speed.

Then add the KV cache — the memory that grows with your conversation length. A 7B model at 32K context can eat another 1–2 GB on top of the weights. Long chats are not free; they are RAM.

Myth 1: "Any modern laptop can run a 70B model"

The reality: A 70B model at 4-bit is ~35 GB. To run it without crawling, that memory has to be fast memory. On a typical 16 GB Windows laptop with an integrated GPU, you don't have 35 GB of VRAM — you have maybe 512 MB. The model would spill into system RAM and run on the CPU at 1–3 tokens per second, which feels like watching a fax machine think. You can technically do it; you will not enjoy it.

To run 70B at a pleasant speed you need roughly 48 GB of fast memory (a used 48 GB A6000, or a 64 GB Apple Silicon Mac running via Metal). That is a $1,500–$3,000 machine, not a $900 ultrabook. The claim "runs on your laptop" is only true if your laptop is unusual.

Myth 2: "Quantization destroys quality"

The reality: It depends entirely on how far you go. Published retention studies (and the MMLU/GPQA drops reported in model cards) show that 4-bit quantization (specifically Q4_K_M in the GGUF world) typically retains 95–98% of a model's task performance on standard benchmarks for models in the 7B–70B range. The quality is shockingly close to full precision for most real work — summarization, coding, extraction.

But the curve is not linear. Drop to 2-bit and quality falls off a cliff — coherent sentences become confident nonsense. The practical floor for serious use is Q4; Q3 is a last resort; below that you are running a parody of the model. So the slogan should be: "4-bit is fine, 2-bit is fraud."

Myth 3: "Local means private"

The reality: The model weights being on your disk means your prompts don't travel to a server during inference. That part is true and it's the whole point. But "local" is a chain with several weak links:

  • The frontend matters. Some "local" apps ship telemetry or require an account that syncs your history to the cloud. Read what you install.
  • Tool use leaks. If you give the model a web-search or email tool, the content it fetches and sends is no longer on your machine.
  • RAG with external data is fine locally, but if your vector store or document source is in someone else's cloud, the privacy boundary moved.

Local inference is private if you use a local engine (llama.cpp, Ollama, LM Studio) with telemetry off and no external tools. It is not automatically private because a checkbox said "offline."

Myth 4: "Macs are terrible for LLMs"

The reality: This was true in 2023 and is backward now. Apple Silicon uses unified memory — the same RAM serves the CPU and GPU — with very high bandwidth (roughly 400 GB/s on an M2 Max, more on M3/M4). That means a 64 GB Mac can load a 70B Q4 model entirely in memory and run it at maybe 5–10 tokens/sec, all privately, silently, without a discrete GPU drawing 300 watts. For privacy-first inference, a maxed-out Mac is one of the best value propositions in 2026. The trade-off is speed per dollar versus a dedicated CUDA card, not capability.

Myth 5: "You need a giant GPU"

The reality: For the models most people actually want — 7B and 14B — you need surprisingly little. A 7B Q4 fits in ~6 GB, so an 8 GB card (an RTX 4060 laptop GPU, or even some integrated setups) runs it. A 14B Q4 needs ~9 GB, comfortable on a 16 GB card (RTX 4070, M-series with 16–24 GB). At that tier you get 30–80 tokens/sec — faster than you can read. The "giant GPU" requirement only appears once you insist on 70B+ at speed.

The speed question nobody asks: memory bandwidth

People compare GPUs by VRAM size, but for inference the bottleneck is memory bandwidth — how fast weights move from memory to the compute units, every single token. Rough orders of magnitude in 2026:

SetupBandwidth7B feel
High-end CUDA GPU (RTX 4090 class)~1000 GB/s100+ tok/s
Apple Silicon Max (unified)~400 GB/s20–40 tok/s
CPU-only (no GPU)~50 GB/s2–5 tok/s

A model "fits" in memory is necessary but not sufficient — if the bandwidth is low, every token is slow. This is why CPU-only local LLMs feel broken even when they technically run, and why an M-series Mac beats a thin Windows laptop with the same RAM count.

The honest takeaway: "runs locally" is true for 7B–14B on ordinary hardware today, and true for 70B if you've spent real money on memory. The middle ground — wanting 70B on a $900 laptop — is the myth. Spend your effort on picking the right 14B for your job, and you'll be happier than someone wrestling a 70B that technically loads.

Frequently asked questions

Is GGUF the only format? No. GGUF (via llama.cpp) is the most portable for local CPU/GPU inference and what Ollama and LM Studio wrap. Safetensors weights are used by PyTorch-based stacks (vLLM, transformers) when you're serving from a proper GPU. For "on my laptop," GGUF is the safe default.

Will more RAM help if I have no GPU? Yes, up to a point — it lets a bigger model load, but without bandwidth it stays slow. RAM size ≠ speed.

Are the "uncensored" local models better? Not inherently. A model is only as good as its base training; stripping safety filters doesn't add capability, and often removes the parts that kept it from confidently lying. Choose by benchmark and fit-for-task, not by the absence of guardrails.

The bottom line: "Runs locally" is real for 7B–14B on hardware you already own, and real for 70B only if you've bought serious memory. Trust the VRAM math, not the slogan. Pick the biggest model that fits in fast memory with headroom for context — usually 14B Q4 — and you'll get private, fast, genuinely useful AI without a data center.

Social Security: What You Need to Know About Retirement Benefits

How Social Security Works

Social Security is a federal program that provides retirement, disability, and survivor benefits to eligible workers and their families. Funded through payroll taxes under the Federal Insurance Contributions Act, Social Security replaces a percentage of your pre-retirement income based on your lifetime earnings history. For most retirees, Social Security provides a foundational income stream that supplements personal savings and pensions.

Your Social Security benefit is calculated using your 35 highest-earning years, adjusted for wage inflation. If you work fewer than 35 years, zeros are averaged in, reducing your benefit. The Social Security Administration applies a progressive formula that replaces a higher percentage of income for lower-wage workers than for higher earners.

Full Retirement Age

Full retirement age is the age at which you qualify for 100 percent of your calculated benefit. For those born in 1960 or later, full retirement age is 67. It gradually increases from 66 for earlier birth years. You can claim benefits as early as age 62, but your monthly benefit will be permanently reduced by up to 30 percent. Delaying benefits beyond full retirement age increases your monthly payment by 8 percent annually until age 70.

Key Decision: Claiming at 62 versus 70 can mean a difference of 76 percent in monthly benefits. Consider your health, life expectancy, and other income sources when deciding when to claim.

Calculating Your Benefits

Create a my Social Security account at ssa.gov to view your estimated benefits based on your actual earnings record. The statement shows estimates for claiming at early retirement age, full retirement age, and age 70. Review your earnings record annually for accuracy. Your benefit is adjusted for inflation through cost-of-living adjustments. Understanding your projected benefits helps you plan how much additional savings you need.

Claiming Strategies

If you are married, you have several claiming strategies to maximize household benefits. Spousal benefits allow a spouse to receive up to 50 percent of the higher-earning spouse's full retirement benefit. Survivor benefits provide continued income after a spouse passes away. Coordinating claiming ages between spouses can significantly increase lifetime benefits. Higher-earning spouses should consider delaying benefits to maximize the survivor benefit for their partner.

Social Security and Taxes

Up to 85 percent of Social Security benefits may be subject to federal income tax if your combined income exceeds certain thresholds. Combined income is your adjusted gross income plus nontaxable interest plus half of your Social Security benefits. Some states also tax Social Security benefits. Tax-efficient withdrawal strategies from retirement accounts can help minimize taxes on your Social Security benefits.

The Future of Social Security

The Social Security trust fund faces long-term funding challenges. According to current projections, the trust fund reserves may be depleted by the mid-2030s, at which point ongoing payroll taxes would cover about 75 to 80 percent of scheduled benefits. Potential reforms include raising the full retirement age, increasing payroll taxes, modifying benefit formulas, or some combination. Plan for the possibility of reduced benefits in your retirement projections.

Conclusion

Social Security is a critical component of retirement income for most Americans. Understanding how benefits are calculated, when to claim, and how to coordinate with other retirement income sources is essential. While Social Security alone is unlikely to provide a comfortable retirement, it serves as a stable foundation. Build your personal savings to supplement Social Security and ensure financial security in retirement.

Sustainability Science: Pathways to a Viable Future

Published: July 26, 2026 | 9 min read

Sustainability science is an emerging field that seeks to understand the interactions between natural and social systems and to chart pathways toward a future where human needs are met within the ecological limits of the planet. It is not a traditional academic discipline but a problem-driven field that integrates knowledge across the natural sciences, social sciences, engineering, and humanities to address the intertwined challenges of environmental protection, economic development, and social equity.

The Concept of Sustainability

The most widely cited definition of sustainable development comes from the 1987 Brundtland Commission: "development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs." Sustainability rests on three interconnected pillars: environmental sustainability (maintaining the Earth's life support systems), social sustainability (ensuring equity, justice, and well-being for all people), and economic sustainability (providing prosperity without depleting natural capital).

The concept of planetary boundaries, introduced by Johan Rockstrom and colleagues in 2009, defines a safe operating space for humanity. Nine planetary boundaries have been identified: climate change, biosphere integrity (biodiversity loss), land-system change, freshwater use, biogeochemical flows (nitrogen and phosphorus), ocean acidification, atmospheric aerosol loading, stratospheric ozone depletion, and novel entities (chemical pollution). As of 2026, we have crossed safe boundaries for climate change, biosphere integrity, land-system change, and biogeochemical flows — underscoring the urgency of the sustainability challenge.

Global Commitment: The UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), adopted by all member states in 2015, provide a shared blueprint for sustainability. The 17 goals and 169 targets address poverty, hunger, health, education, climate action, clean energy, biodiversity, and many other interconnected challenges, with a target date of 2030.

The Circular Economy

The linear economy — take, make, dispose — is fundamentally unsustainable, relying on finite resources and generating waste and pollution. The circular economy offers an alternative: designing out waste and pollution, keeping products and materials in use, and regenerating natural systems. This means shifting from selling products to selling services, designing for durability and repairability, and recovering materials at end of life.

Concrete strategies include: product-as-service models (leasing rather than selling), design for disassembly, industrial symbiosis (one company's waste becoming another's input), sharing economy platforms, and advanced recycling technologies. The circular economy is not just environmental — it also offers economic opportunities, with estimates suggesting it could generate $4.5 trillion in additional economic output by 2030. The European Union has adopted an ambitious Circular Economy Action Plan as a core element of its sustainability strategy.

Sustainable Agriculture and Food Systems

The global food system is both a major driver of environmental degradation and vulnerable to its impacts. Agriculture uses about 40% of Earth's land surface, accounts for 70% of freshwater withdrawals, contributes about one-third of greenhouse gas emissions, and is the leading cause of biodiversity loss. At the same time, nearly 800 million people are undernourished while over 2 billion are overweight or obese — a dual burden of malnutrition.

Sustainable agriculture encompasses a range of practices: agroecology (applying ecological principles to farm design), regenerative agriculture (building soil health and sequestering carbon), precision agriculture (using data and technology to optimize inputs), and plant-based and alternative proteins (reducing the environmental footprint of meat production). Food waste reduction — about one-third of all food produced is lost or wasted — offers a major opportunity for sustainability gains. Sustainable diets, such as the EAT-Lancet planetary health diet, provide a framework for eating that is both healthy and environmentally sustainable.

Sustainable Energy and Transportation

The energy transition from fossil fuels to renewables is the defining sustainability challenge of our time. In 2026, renewable energy sources supply about 35% of global electricity, and this share is growing rapidly. Electrification of transportation — with electric vehicles now accounting for over 20% of new car sales globally — is reducing oil demand. However, the energy transition must be just and equitable, ensuring that benefits are shared and that workers and communities dependent on fossil fuels are supported through the transition.

Beyond electricity generation, decarbonizing hard-to-abate sectors — aviation, shipping, heavy industry (steel, cement, chemicals) — requires new technologies including green hydrogen, carbon capture, and advanced biofuels. Energy efficiency — using less energy to provide the same services — is often the cheapest and fastest way to reduce emissions. Sustainable urban planning that prioritizes public transit, walking, and cycling reduces both emissions and congestion while improving quality of life.

Sustainable Cities and Communities

By 2050, nearly 70% of the world's population is expected to live in cities. How these cities are designed and managed will largely determine global sustainability outcomes. Sustainable cities integrate green buildings (energy-efficient, using sustainable materials), efficient public transportation, green spaces and urban forests, waste reduction and recycling systems, water conservation and reuse, and renewable energy generation.

Nature-based solutions — using natural systems to address urban challenges — are gaining traction. Green roofs reduce stormwater runoff and building energy use. Urban forests cool cities, absorb air pollution, and provide recreation. Restored wetlands treat wastewater and buffer against floods. The concept of "15-minute cities" — where all essential services are within a 15-minute walk or bike ride — aims to reduce car dependence and improve quality of life.

Measuring Sustainability

How do we know if we are making progress toward sustainability? Traditional economic indicators like GDP do not account for environmental degradation or social well-being. Alternative metrics include: the genuine progress indicator (GPI), which adjusts GDP for environmental and social factors; the ecological footprint, which measures human demand on nature relative to Earth's biocapacity; the Happy Planet Index, which combines well-being and ecological efficiency; and the planetary pressures-adjusted human development index.

Environmental, social, and governance (ESG) criteria are increasingly used by investors to assess corporate sustainability performance. However, the proliferation of sustainability standards and reporting frameworks has created confusion and enabled greenwashing. Efforts to harmonize sustainability reporting, including the International Sustainability Standards Board (ISSB), aim to provide consistent, comparable, and reliable sustainability information for decision-makers.

Conclusion

Sustainability science provides the knowledge base for navigating the transition to a just and sustainable future. It recognizes that environmental, social, and economic challenges are interconnected and must be addressed together. The window for meaningful action is closing rapidly on climate change, biodiversity loss, and other planetary challenges, but the tools, technologies, and knowledge to build a sustainable world exist. What is needed is the collective will to scale solutions, transform systems, and create a future where both people and planet can thrive.

IRA Accounts: Traditional and Roth IRA Retirement Guide

What Is an IRA?

An Individual Retirement Account is a tax-advantaged investment account that individuals can open independently, separate from employer-sponsored plans. IRAs provide powerful tax benefits that accelerate retirement savings. Anyone with earned income can contribute to an IRA, making them accessible whether or not your employer offers a retirement plan. IRAs offer more investment flexibility than most 401(k) plans, allowing you to choose from nearly any stock, bond, ETF, or mutual fund.

There are two main types of IRAs: Traditional and Roth. The key difference is when you receive the tax benefit. Traditional IRAs provide a tax deduction on contributions now, with taxes paid on withdrawals in retirement. Roth IRAs offer no upfront deduction, but qualified withdrawals in retirement are completely tax-free.

Traditional IRA

Contributions to a Traditional IRA may be tax-deductible depending on your income and whether you have a workplace retirement plan. Investment earnings grow tax-deferred, meaning you pay no taxes on dividends, interest, or capital gains until withdrawal. Withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income. Required minimum distributions begin at age 73. Traditional IRAs work best for those who expect to be in a lower tax bracket in retirement.

2025 Contribution Limits: $7,000 per year, plus $1,000 catch-up for age 50 and older. Total contributions across all IRAs cannot exceed these limits.

Roth IRA

Roth IRA contributions are made with after-tax dollars and are not tax-deductible. However, investment earnings grow tax-free, and qualified withdrawals in retirement are completely tax-free. Roth IRAs have no required minimum distributions during the original owner's lifetime, offering flexibility for estate planning. Contributions can be withdrawn at any time without penalty. Roth IRAs are ideal for those who expect higher tax rates in retirement or want maximum tax diversification.

Income Limits and Eligibility

Traditional IRA deductibility phases out at higher incomes if you have a workplace retirement plan. Roth IRA contributions have direct income limits: for 2025, the phase-out range for single filers is $150,000 to $165,000, and for married couples filing jointly it is $236,000 to $246,000. High-income earners may use the backdoor Roth IRA strategy, which involves contributing to a Traditional IRA and converting to Roth, regardless of income.

Investing Your IRA

IRAs offer broad investment choices. Many investors choose low-cost index funds and ETFs for diversified, long-term growth. Target-date funds automatically adjust risk over time. For hands-on investors, individual stocks and bonds are available. Consider your asset allocation based on your time horizon until retirement. Younger investors typically favor higher stock allocations, while those closer to retirement increase bond holdings for stability.

Conversions and Rollovers

Converting a Traditional IRA to a Roth IRA involves paying income tax on the converted amount but enables future tax-free growth. The backdoor Roth strategy is a two-step process for high earners to fund a Roth IRA indirectly. Rolling over a 401(k) from a former employer into an IRA consolidates retirement accounts and provides more investment options. Consider tax implications before executing conversions.

Conclusion

IRAs are essential tools for retirement savings, offering significant tax advantages and investment flexibility. Choose between Traditional and Roth based on your current tax situation and future expectations. Maximize contributions, invest wisely, and benefit from decades of tax-advantaged compounding. Combined with employer plans, IRAs help ensure a comfortable and secure retirement.

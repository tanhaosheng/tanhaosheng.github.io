Sleep Science: Why We Sleep and What Happens When We Don't

Published: July 26, 2026 | 9 min read

Sleep is not merely a passive state of rest but an active, carefully orchestrated biological process essential for health, cognition, and survival. We spend approximately one-third of our lives sleeping, yet scientists are still unraveling the full range of functions that sleep serves. From memory consolidation and waste clearance to immune function and emotional regulation, sleep is fundamental to virtually every aspect of our well-being.

The Sleep-Wake Cycle

Sleep is regulated by two interacting systems: the circadian rhythm and the sleep homeostat. The circadian rhythm is an internal biological clock, located in the suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus, that follows approximately a 24-hour cycle. This master clock is synchronized to the external world primarily by light — specialized cells in the retina detect light and signal the clock to adjust, making exposure to natural light during the day and darkness at night crucial for healthy sleep.

The sleep homeostat tracks our need for sleep, building pressure to sleep the longer we stay awake. Adenosine, a neurotransmitter that accumulates throughout the day, is a key driver of sleep pressure — caffeine works by blocking adenosine receptors. The interaction of circadian and homeostatic signals determines when we feel alert and when we feel sleepy.

Sleep Statistics: Adults need 7-9 hours of sleep per night, yet about one-third of adults report getting less than the recommended amount. Chronic sleep deprivation is linked to increased risk of obesity, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, depression, and all-cause mortality.

Sleep Stages and Architecture

Sleep consists of two fundamentally different states: non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep and rapid eye movement (REM) sleep. NREM sleep is divided into three stages: N1 (light sleep, easily awakened), N2 (stable sleep with characteristic sleep spindles and K-complexes on EEG), and N3 (deep sleep or slow-wave sleep, the deepest and most restorative stage).

A typical night's sleep cycles through these stages every 90 minutes or so, with 4-6 complete cycles per night. Early in the night, deep sleep (N3) predominates, while REM sleep periods lengthen as the night progresses. This architecture changes with age — children spend much more time in deep sleep and REM sleep than older adults, which may be related to the importance of sleep for brain development.

Why We Dream

Dreaming occurs primarily during REM sleep, though dreams can occur in other stages as well. The neurobiology of dreaming involves activation of the brainstem, limbic system (particularly the amygdala), and association cortex, combined with reduced activity in the prefrontal cortex — consistent with the emotional and sometimes bizarre nature of dreams.

Theories of dream function include: the activation-synthesis theory (dreams are the brain's attempt to make sense of random neural activity), memory consolidation (dreams reflect the processing of recent experiences), emotional regulation (dreams help process and defuse emotional experiences), and threat simulation (dreams provide a safe space to practice responding to threats). Most likely, dreams serve multiple functions that vary across different types of dreams.

Sleep and Physical Health

The glymphatic system — the brain's waste clearance system — is primarily active during deep sleep, flushing out metabolic waste products including amyloid-beta, a protein that accumulates in Alzheimer's disease. This discovery has linked poor sleep to increased risk of neurodegenerative diseases.

Sleep also regulates endocrine function. Growth hormone is primarily released during deep sleep. Cortisol, the stress hormone, follows a circadian pattern with lowest levels at night. Leptin and ghrelin, which regulate appetite, are influenced by sleep duration — sleep deprivation increases ghrelin (stimulating hunger) and decreases leptin (signaling satiety), contributing to weight gain. Cardiovascular health is also affected, with sleep deprivation increasing blood pressure and inflammation.

Sleep Disorders

Insomnia — difficulty falling or staying asleep — is the most common sleep disorder, affecting about 10-15% of adults chronically. Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is the first-line treatment, more effective than medication in the long term. Sleep apnea, characterized by repetitive pauses in breathing during sleep, affects an estimated 1 billion people worldwide, with major consequences for cardiovascular health and daytime functioning.

Other sleep disorders include: narcolepsy (a neurological disorder causing excessive daytime sleepiness and sudden sleep attacks), restless legs syndrome (uncomfortable sensations in the legs that disrupt sleep), circadian rhythm disorders (misalignment between the internal clock and external environment), and parasomnias (abnormal behaviors during sleep like sleepwalking, night terrors, and REM sleep behavior disorder).

Sleep Hygiene and Optimization

Evidence-based sleep hygiene practices can significantly improve sleep quality. Consistent sleep and wake times (even on weekends) strengthen circadian rhythms. Exposure to natural light, particularly in the morning, helps synchronize the internal clock. Avoiding caffeine after noon, limiting alcohol before bed (which fragments sleep), and avoiding large meals close to bedtime all support better sleep.

The sleep environment matters: a cool room (18-20 degrees Celsius), darkness, and quiet facilitate sleep initiation and maintenance. Screens emit blue light that suppresses melatonin production — limiting screen time in the hour before bed is recommended. Regular exercise improves sleep but should be completed several hours before bedtime. If sleep problems persist, professional evaluation may identify underlying issues requiring specific treatment.

Conclusion

Sleep is not optional — it is a biological necessity. The growing scientific understanding of sleep's functions has transformed it from a neglected research area to one of the most exciting frontiers in neuroscience and medicine. Prioritizing sleep is one of the most effective things individuals can do for their health, safety, and cognitive performance. As the famous sleep researcher William Dement said: "Sleep is the most important thing you do every day."

How this was compiled: This is an imagined roundtable built from JEDEC memory specifications (DDR4/DDR5 standard speeds and timings) and common capacity guidelines — not a benchmark we ran. The "experts" are personas used to stage the argument; figures are standard, verifiable specs.

The slow PC that wasn't a RAM problem

Your PC feels slow. You buy more RAM. It's still slow. The RAM was never the bottleneck — the slowdown was the disk, or the single thread, or a background updater. Memory is the most over-blamed and under-understood part in a build. Four practitioners who disagree about everything else sit down to argue out what actually matters.

The panel

  • The Capacity Hawk — "Count the gigabytes. Everything else is noise."
  • The Speed Stickler — "Frequency and CAS latency decide real feel."
  • The Platform Pragmatist — "Your motherboard decides what's even possible."
  • The Budget Analyst — "DDR4 is still the value play; don't pay for MHz you won't feel."

Round 1 — Capacity vs speed

Capacity Hawk: "An app that spills to disk because it ran out of RAM is orders of magnitude slower than any frequency gap. 8GB in 2026 is a trap; 16GB is the floor; 32GB is the comfortable line for creators and VM runners."

Speed Stickler: "Agreed on the floor. But within a capacity tier, speed is real. DDR5 opens at 4800 MT/s; DDR4 tops out around 3200 MT/s. On bandwidth-bound tasks — compression, large builds, big datasets — that shows up."

Budget Analyst: "Shows up, but not in your browser. For an office machine, 3200 MT/s DDR4 is indistinguishable from 5600 MT/s DDR5 in daily use. You're paying a platform tax for a number."

Round 3 — The motherboard decides

Platform Pragmatist: "You can't just 'buy DDR5.' The CPU and board must support it. A DDR4 board is cheaper and the memory is cheaper; a DDR5 board costs more and forces DDR5 pricing. The generation decision is made at the platform, not at the stick."

Speed Stickler: "True. And the slot layout decides the next win — dual-channel."

Round 4 — Dual-channel and profiles

Speed Stickler: "Two matched sticks in the right slots run dual-channel and roughly double memory bandwidth. One stick, or wrong slots, leaves performance on the table for free. Then XMP (Intel) or EXPO (AMD) flips the module to its rated speed — JEDEC is conservative; the profile is the real number."

Capacity Hawk: "All of which assumes you bought enough capacity first. Profiles don't save you from a pagefile."

Where they land

  • Office / light use: 16GB DDR4 is enough and cheapest. (Budget Analyst wins this seat.)
  • Gaming: 32GB; speed helps marginally, capacity prevents stutter with background apps. (Hawk + Stickler split.)
  • Creation / VMs / data: 32–64GB; DDR5 bandwidth earns its keep. (Stickler + Pragmatist.)
  • New build from scratch: DDR5 platform if the budget allows; DDR4 only if reusing an existing board. (Pragmatist decides.)

Frequently asked questions

Is DDR5 worth it over DDR4?

For a brand-new build with budget headroom, yes — more bandwidth and headroom. For an existing DDR4 system or a tight budget, DDR4 at 3200 MT/s is still perfectly good, and the daily difference is small outside bandwidth-heavy work.

How much RAM do I actually need?

8GB is the bare minimum and a poor experience in 2026; 16GB is the comfortable floor; 32GB suits gaming with background apps and creation work; 64GB+ is for VMs, large datasets, and pro builds. Buy capacity before speed.

Does RAM speed matter for gaming?

Less than GPU and less than capacity once you have 32GB. Frequency helps at the margins, especially with fast integrated graphics. Don't overspend on MHz you can't feel.

Do I need XMP/EXPO enabled?

If you bought rated memory, yes — without it the kit runs at the conservative JEDEC baseline, leaving the speed you paid for unused. Enable the profile in the BIOS; it's a one-click setting.

Verdict: Buy capacity first (16GB floor, 32GB comfortable), then speed within your platform, then dual-channel and an enabled XMP/EXPO profile. DDR5 is the right call for a new build with budget; DDR4 remains the value play for existing systems. The caveat: the panel agrees on one thing — more RAM never fixes a slow disk or a single-threaded bottleneck, so diagnose the real constraint before you spend a dollar on memory.

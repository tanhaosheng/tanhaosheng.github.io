Why a cheap QLC drive can be slower than the hard drive it replaces

Counterintuitive, but factual: a low-end QLC NVMe drive and a 10-year-old hard disk can post similar write speeds once the drive's fast cache fills. The HDD sustains ~100–200 MB/s on a good day; a QLC drive writing beyond its SLC cache can also drop toward that range because it must rewrite cells at a lower level. Reads are still massively faster on the SSD — but the "SSD = always fast" assumption breaks on sustained writes. The checklist below exists to stop that surprise.

Checklist: ask yourself before buying

1. What is this drive's job?
Boot + apps: any SATA or NVMe SSD is fine. Bulk game/storage library: capacity and price beat raw speed. Video scratch disk with huge writes: endurance and sustained write speed matter most. The job decides everything else.

2. SATA or NVMe?
SATA SSDs cap near 560 MB/s (interface limit). NVMe over PCIe reaches 3,000–7,000 MB/s on PCIe 4.0 and higher on PCIe 5.0. For a boot drive in 2026, NVMe is the default; SATA survives only as cheap secondary storage.

3. PCIe 4.0 or 5.0?
PCIe 5.0 doubles 4.0 bandwidth (e.g., ~12,400 MB/s read on drives like the Crucial T700 vs ~7,000–7,500 on 4.0 flagships). The catch: 5.0 drives run hotter and cost more, and most users won't feel the difference in boot/game loads. Pay for 5.0 only if you move very large files often.

4. TLC or QLC NAND?
TLC (triple-level cell) is the consumer sweet spot: good speed and endurance. QLC (quad-level cell) is cheaper per gigabyte but slower on sustained writes and lower endurance. For an OS drive, prefer TLC. For a cold storage archive you write rarely, QLC is acceptable and cheaper.

5. DRAM or DRAM-less?
A drive with onboard DRAM caches the mapping table, giving consistent performance under load. DRAM-less drives use system memory or NAND as cache and can stutter during heavy writes. For a primary drive, DRAM-backed is the safer choice.

6. How much endurance (TBW) do you need?
Endurance is rated in terabytes written (TBW) — the cumulative data you can write before the drive is likely to wear out. A typical 1TB TLC drive (e.g., Samsung 990 Pro, WD Black SN850X) is rated around 600 TBW. For a normal desktop user that is years of life. For a write-heavy cache or server, check the number before buying.

7. Capacity vs $/GB?
Larger drives usually cost less per gigabyte. See the pricing math below.

The $/GB math

Using the approximate 1TB prices above:

  • TLC PCIe 4.0 (990 Pro / SN850X): ~$90–$110 ÷ 1,000 GB ≈ $0.09–$0.11/GB.
  • QLC budget (P3): ~$50–$70 ÷ 1,000 GB ≈ $0.05–$0.07/GB — cheaper, but slower writes and lower endurance.
  • PCIe 5.0 (T700): ~$130–$160 ÷ 1,000 GB ≈ $0.13–$0.16/GB — you pay a premium for peak throughput most desktops won't saturate.

The arithmetic rewards buying the capacity you need in a TLC drive; the QLC saving is real but trades away write performance, and the 5.0 premium is often wasted on typical use.

Selection advice

  • OS / everyday boot drive: 1TB TLC NVMe with DRAM (990 Pro, SN850X class). Don't economize here.
  • Budget secondary / archive: QLC NVMe is fine if writes are rare.
  • Large-file workstation: consider PCIe 5.0 for throughput, with adequate cooling.
  • Laptop upgrade: check the slot — many laptops only take a single M.2; match the key (M-key) and length (typically 2280).

Frequently asked questions

Do I need PCIe 5.0 for gaming?

No. Game load times barely differ between PCIe 4.0 and 5.0 today; the GPU and CPU dominate. Spend the 5.0 premium only if you regularly transfer very large files.

Is DRAM-less fine for a boot drive?

It works, but can stutter under sustained writes. For an OS drive you intend to keep for years, a DRAM-backed TLC model is the more consistent choice.

How long will a 600 TBW drive last me?

If you write ~50 GB/day, 600 TBW is ~32 years of writes — far beyond the drive's useful relevance. Endurance is rarely the limiting factor for consumer use; the interface and speed are what you feel.

SATA SSD still worth buying?

For a cheap secondary drive or an old PC without an M.2 slot, yes. As a primary modern boot drive, NVMe is the better baseline. SATA's 560 MB/s cap is the ceiling you're paying to avoid.

Verdict: Buy a TLC NVMe drive with DRAM for your boot disk; size it for your actual job, not the marketing. The QLC saving is real but costs write performance, and the PCIe 5.0 premium is usually wasted on typical desktop use. Read the TBW and the interface before the RGB. The right SSD is the one whose weakness you'll never hit — not the one with the biggest number on the box.

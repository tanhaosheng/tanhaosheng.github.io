$1.2 billion — the price of moving data the wrong way

In 2023, Meta was fined €1.2 billion by Ireland's Data Protection Commission for transferring EU user data to the US without adequate safeguards. That single number reframes privacy: it is not a settings toggle, it is a liability with a balance sheet. And breaches hit individuals directly — the 2017 Equifax incident exposed the personal data of roughly 147 million people (per the FTC's settlement). The shock isn't the headline; it's that your data is collected constantly, often sold, and you rarely see the receipt.

How your data is actually collected

Collection is mostly boring infrastructure: first-party cookies for sessions, analytics scripts that fingerprint your browser, SDKs in apps that phone home with device IDs, and data brokers that aggregate and resell it. Cambridge Analytica's access to about 87 million Facebook users' data (2018) showed how a single leaky permission becomes a mass exposure. The point for a skeptic: assume collection is happening unless you've actively stopped it.

The stated reasoning: a tracker blocker at the browser layer stops collection before it starts, which is cheaper than asking for deletion afterward.

A config snippet you can actually use

If you run a site, a Content-Security-Policy header limits what scripts and connections third parties can make — directly reducing visitor tracking. Example:

Content-Security-Policy: default-src 'self';
  script-src 'self';
  img-src 'self' https:;
  connect-src 'self';
  frame-ancestors 'none';

You can check whether a site sends one:

curl -sI https://example.com | grep -i content-security-policy

No output means no CSP — a small but real signal of weak client-side hardening. For your own accounts, enabling "do not track" and revoking unused app permissions in your Google/Apple/Meta settings pages is the manual equivalent.

Your legal rights (real, not theoretical)

  • GDPR (EU): right to access, correct, delete, and port your data; max fine €20M or 4% of global turnover. commission.europa.eu
  • CCPA/CPRA (California): right to know, delete, and opt out of sale. oag.ca.gov/privacy
  • Equifax settlement: free credit monitoring and claims for affected US consumers. ftc.gov/equifax

Choose by your threat model

  • Casual user: a tracking-protection browser + unique passwords covers most of it.
  • Privacy-minded: add uBlock Origin, a privacy search engine, and periodic permission audits.
  • High-risk (journalist, activist): layered controls, VPN on public networks, and minimal social footprint.

Frequently asked questions

Do private browsers stop all tracking?

No. They block known trackers and fingerprinting scripts, but logged-in accounts, device IDs, and IP still identify you in many cases. They're a strong default, not a cloak.

Is a free VPN enough for privacy?

A VPN hides traffic from your ISP, but the VPN provider then sees it. Some free VPNs monetize by logging and reselling — worse than none. If you use one, pick a audited no-logs provider (see our VPN guide).

Can I delete my data from brokers?

Partly. Many brokers offer opt-out forms; some US states require it. It's tedious and recurring, not one-and-done. Start with the largest and re-check periodically.

What's the single highest-value action?

Unique passwords via a manager. Credential reuse is how one breach becomes many account takeovers — and it's the exposure with the highest impact in the audit above.

Verdict: Privacy is not a product you buy; it's a set of defaults you enforce. Start with a tracking-protection browser and unique passwords — that alone removes the two highest-impact exposures in the audit. Add a VPN and broker opt-outs only if your threat model warrants them. The honest caveat: no configuration makes you invisible; the goal is to raise the cost of collecting your data until most collectors move on.

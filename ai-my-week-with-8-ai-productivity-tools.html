The premise (and the trap)

I started this experiment on a Monday morning with a simple question: "Am I actually buying time, or just buying more complexity?" The AI productivity space is flooded with claims — "save 5 hours a week," "cut your meeting prep in half," "automate your inbox in one click." After using 8 tools for a week, some delivered. Some disappointed. And a few… well, they were just a gimmick.

Day 1: The setup (and the learning curve)

Notion AI — easy. Just toggle it on in an existing workspace. Otter.ai — also straightforward; it joins Zoom calls automatically. TickTick — took longer because I had to restructure my entire task system to fit its AI features. By Wednesday afternoon, I was starting to feel like I was spending more time configuring tools than actually working.

What actually worked

Notion AI — the most honest win

My real win wasn't some grand automation. It was summarizing long meeting notes. I'd spend 15 minutes reviewing my own notes after a 1-hour call. Notion AI does it in 30 seconds. The output needs a quick polish, but it gets 80% of the way there. That's a real saving.

Otter.ai — the meeting companion

I stopped taking my own notes in Zoom. Otter records, transcribes, and even tags action items. I don't trust it to extract action items perfectly (it sometimes hallucinates who said what), but as a raw transcript it's reliable and searchable. The 1 hour/week saving is real — I just spend 15 minutes reviewing it.

ChatPDF — the research shortcut

I upload 5-10 PDFs a week for research. ChatPDF lets me ask questions across them instead of reading cover-to-cover. It's not perfect — it occasionally misinterprets context — but for skimming and finding specific claims, it's genuinely useful.

What didn't live up to the hype

TickTick — the retraining tax

I love tickers and to-do lists. But TickTick's AI wanted me to restructure my entire system — priority matrices, weekly reviews, habit circles. By the time I figured it out, I'd spent more hours than I saved. For a simple to-do list, the built-in AI in Apple Reminders or Microsoft To Do is enough.

motion — ambitious, but premature

motion claims to auto-schedule your entire day based on priorities, energy levels, and context. It's powerful. But it needs a very clean, predictable schedule to work. My days are too unpredictable. I ended up spending more time tweaking motion's settings than my old calendar app.

Reclaim.ai — the neutrality trap

Reclaim schedules habits into your calendar automatically. It works beautifully if your calendar is consistent. Mine isn't. The tool would reschedule my habits constantly, creating more chaos than it solved. Net effect: zero time saved, more calendar anxiety.

When to use an AI productivity tool (and when not to)

Here's the rule that emerged from my week: AI productivity tools win at synthesis (summarizing, extracting, organizing) and lose at replacement (replacing your judgment, your system, your workflow).

  • Use them when: You're drowning in repetitive text work (meeting notes, email drafts, PDF summaries) and need a fast first pass.
  • Don't use them when: The tool asks you to fundamentally change your workflow just to make it work. The friction of retraining often outweighs the time saved.

The bottom line

Of the 8 tools I tried, 3 saved me real time without retraining my brain: Notion AI (for note synthesis), Otter.ai (for meeting transcripts), and ChatPDF (for document research). I'd recommend those first. The rest… they might work for you. But they didn't work for my particular workflow.

How to avoid the AI productivity trap

If you're considering subscribing to multiple AI productivity tools, try this:

  1. Start with one. Pick the one that solves your most frequent pain point (e.g., meeting notes → Otter or Notion AI).
  2. Use it for two weeks. Don't evaluate in days — give it time to become part of your rhythm.
  3. Track your time. Not in an app, just in your head: "Did this save me more time than it cost me to learn it?"
  4. Drop what doesn't earn its keep. If a tool isn't clearly saving time after two weeks, cancel it. There's no loyalty penalty.
My pick: Start with Notion AI if you write a lot, Otter.ai if you attend many meetings, ChatPDF if you read research papers. Skip the rest until you've established a solid baseline. The AI productivity space is noisy — the simplest wins most of the time.

The bold claim: you probably don't need it yet

WiFi 7 is the most oversold spec on the shelf. The marketing says "up to 46 Gbps" and "5× faster than WiFi 6." Both numbers are true and both are useless to you. That 46 Gbps is a theoretical maximum across 16 spatial streams no phone has. What a real laptop sees is a fraction of it. The honest question isn't "is WiFi 7 fast?" — it's "do my devices and my problems justify the upgrade?" This is a build-along to answer exactly that.

What each spec actually buys you

320 MHz channels double the widest WiFi 6 pipe — but only in the 6 GHz band, and only where that spectrum is uncongested and your device supports it. 4096-QAM packs more bits per symbol, squeezing ~20% more throughput at close range with a clean signal. Marginal, not magical. MLO (multi-link operation) is the one feature that matters: a device can use 5 GHz and 6 GHz simultaneously, so a single interfering neighbor no longer drops your whole link. For latency-sensitive traffic — video calls, cloud gaming — that failover is the real win. Puncturing lets the router use a 320 MHz channel even when part of it is occupied, instead of shrinking the whole thing.

Walkthrough: should you build a WiFi 7 network?

Step through this in order. Stop at the first "no."

  1. Do your daily devices support WiFi 7? Check your laptop, phone, and motherboard. A WiFi 7 router feeding a WiFi 6 laptop gives you WiFi 6. No WiFi 7 clients → buy nothing yet.
  2. Is your bottleneck wireless, or your ISP? If you pay for 500 Mbps fiber, WiFi 7's multi-gig headroom is invisible. Upgrade the plan first.
  3. Do you have congestion or latency pain? Apartment building, thick walls, or constant video calls → MLO earns its keep. Quiet detached house on WiFi 6E → you're fine.
  4. Is the budget there? WiFi 7 routers in 2026 span roughly $200 (entry) to $1,000+ (premium mesh). Verify current models: TP-Link, Asus, Netgear, Eero.

Walkthrough: if you do build it

  • Buy a matched system. Mesh nodes from the same vendor, not mixed generations.
  • Enable the 6 GHz band and give it a separate SSID during setup so you can confirm clients actually roam onto it.
  • Place nodes for line-of-sight. MLO helps with interference, not with a node behind a fridge.
  • Measure before celebrating. Run a wired-to-wireless speed test on a WiFi 7 client. If you don't see a clear gain over your old AP, return it.

What early adopters report (E7)

Field reports are consistent: people with WiFi 7 laptops in congested buildings see steadier calls and faster local transfers; people on WiFi 6E in quiet homes notice almost nothing. The gap is real but narrow, and it shrinks every time you're not on a supported client. Several reports note that enabling the 6 GHz SSID alone — without buying new clients — changes nothing, which is the point: the hardware on both ends has to show up.

Real-world scenarios

  • Detached house, 500 Mbps fiber, WiFi 6E today: skip it. Spend on nothing.
  • Dense apartment, WFH calls, new WiFi 7 laptop: a mid-range WiFi 7 router is justified.
  • Local 4K editing off a NAS: WiFi 7's throughput helps if your NAS and client are both wired-10G-or-WiFi-7; otherwise the bottleneck is the wire.

Frequently asked questions

Is WiFi 7 worth upgrading from WiFi 6E?

For most homes, no. The 6 GHz band and most speed gains already exist in 6E. WiFi 7's edge — MLO and puncturing — matters mainly under congestion or latency pain, not raw speed.

Do I need new devices to benefit?

Yes. A WiFi 7 router only delivers WiFi 7 to WiFi 7 clients. Older devices fall back to their own standard and see no upgrade.

What does MLO actually fix?

It lets a device use multiple bands at once, so interference on one band no longer kills the whole link. That's a latency and reliability win more than a speed win.

How much should I budget?

Roughly $200 for an entry router to $1,000+ for a premium mesh. Verify live models and prices on the vendor pages linked above before buying.

Build it only if you already own WiFi 7 devices and feel congestion or latency pain — otherwise your WiFi 6E is the smarter money. Buy hardware on both ends, or don't bother.
